# ECC durability review (Ma et al., 2021)

## One-paragraph summary
A narrative review on the durability of **ECC** by authors from CUMT Beijing, Imperial College
London and the University of Canterbury. It covers 94 references. It first restates the crack-width
design idea (average crack width should be designed **< 100 µm**; maximum steady-state crack width =
δ0 at peak bridging stress σ0, Eq. 1, Fig. 2, p. 2) and the role of the fiber–matrix interface (§3).
It then reviews nine durability topics (§4): high temperature, permeability (with self-healing),
frost / salt-frost, salt (sulfate, chloride), shrinkage, acid, abrasion, fatigue, and "other"
(carbonation, water absorption). The paper presents no new data. Its only tables are second-hand:
PE-ECC tensile properties at 20–200 °C (Table 1, Luo et al. 2019), water permeability vs crack width
for ECC and reinforced mortar (Table 2, Lepech & Li 2009), PVA-ECC pore-size distribution (Table 3,
Sahmaran et al. 2012) and fatigue-life S–N models (Table 4). Headline points: ECC durability relies on
tight micro-cracks and self-healing rather than on density (p. 2); PVA-ECC loses strain hardening
above about 200–300 °C, PE-ECC at about 140–200 °C; crack width < 100 µm has little effect on
permeability; most work is at 28 d, on a single exposure, and on conventional PVA/PE-ECC, so
high-strength / ultra-high-ductility / high-strength high-ductility ECC durability is a stated gap
(§5, pp. 9–10).

## Objective / research question
- Summarise "up-to-date achievements in ECC durability research", identify research gaps and give
  future directions (p. 2).
- Durability is discussed through the behaviour of fiber, matrix and fiber–matrix interface under
  environmental exposure (Abstract, p. 1).

## Definition of ECC used by the authors (Reported)
- Concept introduced by V.C. Li in the early 1990s from micromechanics and fracture mechanics
  (p. 1, ref. [1] Li & Kanda 1998).
- "Generally, fiber-reinforced cement-based composites with strain hardening behavior and with an
  ultimate tensile strain capacity exceeding 3% can be called ECC" (p. 1, refs. [2,3]). Strain is
  called "ultimate"; whether at peak stress is not stated.
- Tensile strain capacity "several hundred times" that of plain concrete (p. 1).
- Most ECC use PVA fiber, strain capacity 3–5%; PVA-ECC "could hardly achieve high strength" because
  of PVA hydrophilicity; PE is hydrophobic and suits stronger ECC (p. 1).
- PE-ECC: tensile strain capacity up to **11.98%** with f'c **45.86 MPa** (Yu et al. [7], as cited,
  p. 1) ⚠️ the title of ref. [7] says "tensile capacity up to 8%" — mismatch between text and reference.
- Names: UHPFRCC in Japan (ref. [4]), SHCC in Europe (ref. [5]) (p. 1). The authors treat these as
  the same material class. They do not use "HDC". → [[terminology-hdc-ecc-shcc]]

## Crack width control and interface (§2–3, p. 2–3)
- Average crack width "should be designed to be < 100 µm" to maintain high durability (p. 2, refs.
  [14,15]). → [[crack-width]]
- Maximum steady-state crack width of multiple cracking = δ0, the opening at peak bridging stress σ0.
  Beyond δ0 the crack localises and new cracking stops (p. 2, Fig. 2, after Li et al. 2004 [14]).
  Bridging stress σ(δ) from Vf, df, Lf, orientation Φ, snubbing f, and single-fiber P(δ) (Eq. 1).
  → [[fiber-bridging]] · [[multiple-cracking]] · [[strain-hardening]]
- Interface design should maximise J_tip (crack-tip toughness) for ductility (p. 2) ⚠️ wording as
  printed; usual PSH theory relates J_tip to matrix toughness.
- Higher fiber reinforcement index Vf·Lf/df reduces crack spacing and width (Yu et al. 2018 [13],
  as cited, p. 2). → [[fiber-geometry]]
- PVA: hydroxyl groups give strong chemical bond and fiber rupture; oil coating lowers bond and
  enables multiple cracking (Arain et al. 2019 [18], p. 2). → [[pva-fiber]] · [[fiber-matrix-interface]]
- PVA-ECC strain capacity generally lower than PE-ECC: lower fiber modulus and strength **and**
  stronger (hydrophilic) bond (Yun et al. 2011 [19], as cited, p. 2–3). → [[pe-fiber]]
- Very little research links long-term interface change to durability (p. 2).

## 4.1 High temperature (p. 3–4)
- Normal concrete: spalling starts at 200–350 °C; explosive spalling above (Naus 2006 [20], p. 3).
- PVA fibers eliminated explosive spalling of ECC; higher fly ash content improved high-temperature
  resistance (Sahmaran et al. 2011 [21], p. 3). → [[fly-ash]]
- Change in strain hardening after heating is caused by change in microcrack number. Mild
  temperature (50–100 °C) **enhances** the interface; 200 °C weakens fiber and interface, fibers act
  as defects (Yu et al. 2015 [22], HVFA-ECC, p. 3).
- At 100 °C PVA-ECC strain capacity rose with strain rate; at 150 °C ductility lost and rate effect
  shown (Mechtcherine et al. 2012 [23], p. 3). → [[strain-rate-effects]]
- PVA-ECC debris at 600 °C = 4× that of steel-fiber ECC (Pourfalah 2018 [24], p. 3).
- f'c and stiffness about constant up to 200 °C, then linear decline, falling by > 75% at 800 °C
  (Shang & Lu 2014 [25], Fig. 3, p. 3). → [[compressive-strength]]
- At 200 °C matrix toughness increased while PVA fiber strength degraded; lower bond partly offset
  the tensile loss (Zhang et al. 2020 [26], 20/50/100/200 °C, p. 3). → [[matrix-fracture-toughness]]
- PVA-ECC kept strain hardening at 100 °C; ε 0.5–0.8% at 200 °C; brittle at 300 °C; fibers fully
  decomposed at 400 and 600 °C (Bhat et al. 2014 [27], Fig. 4, p. 3).
- PE-ECC: highest strain capacity at 50 °C (10.74%), almost none at 200 °C (0.021%) (Luo et al.
  2019 [29], Table 1, p. 3–4). Few studies on PE-ECC at high temperature (p. 3).
- PVA-ECC strain hardening kept up to 200 °C, brittle after (Wu & Li 2017 [28], p. 3).
→ [[high-temperature-behaviour]]

## 4.2 Permeability and self-healing (p. 3–4)
- Crack width < 100 µm has little effect on concrete permeability (Wang et al. 1997 [30], p. 3).
- Average ECC crack widths reported in the literature are < 100 µm (Fig. 5, refs. [33–39,7]).
- ECC permeability at 1.5 / 2 / 3% pre-strain: 1.95×10⁻¹⁰ / 3×10⁻¹⁰ / 7.74×10⁻¹⁰ m/s; normalized
  per crack about 10⁻¹² m/s/crack at all levels ("not significant"). Reinforced mortar: 10⁻¹¹ →
  10⁻⁵ m/s (crack width 0 → 500 µm), "up to seven orders of magnitude" (Lepech & Li 2009 [40],
  Table 2, p. 3–5). Similar trend in Yu et al. 2017 [32]. Uncracked ECC 8.18×10⁻¹² m/s; uncracked
  mortar 4.58×10⁻¹¹ m/s.
- Self-healing speed is inversely related to crack width: permeability stabilises in 3–4 d for
  crack width < 60 µm, 7–10 d or longer for > 100 µm (ref. [41] Reinhardt & Jooss 2003, as cited,
  p. 3) ⚠️ ref. [41] is about cracked concrete, not ECC.
- Relative water permeability of ECC fell to 0 after 10 wet–dry cycles (self-healing in water)
  (Ma et al. 2014 [42], p. 3).
- Permeability–crack width relation of single ECC crack fitted by a cubic polynomial
  k(δ) = 4.74×10⁻¹⁴δ³ − 2.93×10⁻¹²δ² + 7.98×10⁻¹¹δ + 2.35×10⁻¹⁰, R² = 0.99 (Liu et al. 2016 [44],
  Fig. 6, p. 4–5); text calls it a "third-order exponential function" ⚠️ units of k on axis are m/s ×10⁻⁸.
- Self-healing effect on permeability fitted by an exponential function (Liu et al. [43,44]).
→ NEW [[self-healing]] · NEW [[permeability]] · [[crack-width]]

## 4.3 Frost (freeze–thaw, salt-frost) (p. 4–5)
- ECC porosity rises with fiber volume and is higher than plain concrete (ref. [45], p. 4) ⚠️ ref.
  [45] is an abrasion paper.
- PVA raised total porosity: ECC1 (FA/C 1.2) 19.2% without PVA → 23.7% with PVA, 23.9% after F-T;
  ECC2 (FA/C 2.2) 24.4% → 27.2%, 27.3% after F-T (Sahmaran et al. 2012 [46], Table 3, p. 5). Pore
  size class units not printed ⚠️ (likely µm).
- Mechanism: freezing water causes microcracks that **release** expansion stress (p. 4).
- After 200 F-T cycles, PVA-ECC loses more strain capacity than PE-ECC (Yun et al. 2011 [19], p. 4).
  → [[tensile-strain-capacity]]
- F-T may reduce matrix strength and bond, which can **raise** strain capacity (ref. [47], p. 4)
  ⚠️ ref. [47] is Li & Lemaire, a cracking-mechanics paper.
- After 300 cycles slag-ECC had lower mass loss and UPV loss than FA-ECC; average crack width rose
  1.5×; unaerated concrete failed at 110 cycles while ECC dynamic modulus rose slightly at 300
  cycles (Özbay et al. 2013 [48], p. 4). → [[ggbfs]]
- Durability factor of ECC > 100 vs 20 for unaerated concrete (Li et al. 2008 [49], p. 4).
- Mortar without PVA: 210 cycles, mass loss 10.7%; PVA-ECC (2% PVA) survived 300 cycles, mass loss
  8% (Sahmaran et al. 2012 [46], p. 4).
- Silica fume and slag improve frost resistance of HVFA-ECC (relative dynamic modulus, Fig. 7);
  after 200 cycles max deflection of FA-ECC rose 3× and toughness rose (Figs. 8–9) (Liu et al.
  2018 [50], p. 5). → [[silica-fume]] · [[ggbfs]] · [[flexural-strength]]
- With de-icing salt, after 50 cycles HVFA-ECC acceptable, mortar seriously damaged; ECC kept
  ε > 3%; 2% pre-strained ECC recovered stiffness fully by self-healing (Sahmaran & Li 2007 [51], p. 5).
- Self-healing under water/salt F-T: recovery about 100% at 0.5–1% pre-strain, limited at 1.5–2%,
  not obvious > 2.5% (Zhu et al. 2012 [52], p. 5). Text says "pre-loaded tensile stress level" but
  values are strains (%) ⚠️.
- Frost resistance in solutions: MgSO₄ (10%) > Na₂SO₄ (10%) > water (Zhao et al. 2020 [53], p. 5).
  ⚠️ order meaning (better or worse) as printed; counter-intuitive, check original.
→ [[durability]]

## 4.4 Salt (sulfate, chloride) (p. 5–6)
- After 200 d in sulfate and sulfate–chloride, ECC tensile strength **increased** (denser matrix,
  better bridging); ε > 2%; average crack width < 50 µm (Liu et al. 2017 [57], Fig. 10, p. 5).
- Self-healing faster and more complete in sulfate (Liu et al. 2017 [58]); similar self-healing in
  high chloride, but chloride lowered first-crack strength and interface bond (matrix toughness,
  friction, chemical bond) (Li & Li 2011 [59], p. 5). → [[fiber-matrix-interface]]
- Chloride effective diffusion coefficient: mortar grows exponentially with pre-load deformation,
  ECC linearly, and ECC is lower at every deformation (Sahmaran et al. 2007 [60], Fig. 11, p. 6).
  Fit printed: ECC y = 24.87x + 2.04 (R² 0.95) ⚠️ axis units ×10⁻¹² m²/s.
- Sulfate mechanism text says "calcium vanadite" and CH "(silicalite)" (p. 5) ⚠️ likely translation
  errors for ettringite / portlandite.

## 4.5 Shrinkage (p. 6–7)
- 28-d shrinkage of ECC 1200–1800 µε vs plain concrete 400–800 µε (high binder content) (Yang
  et al. 2007 [61], p. 6). Printed "le" = µε.
- Low-drying-shrinkage ECC: ε about 2.5%, 28-d shrinkage 109–242 µε; no visible cracks in ring test
  (Zhang et al. 2013 [62], p. 6).
- Shrinkage increases with W/B, decreases with sand/binder ratio and fly ash; fiber volume no
  significant effect (Gao et al. 2018 [66], p. 6). → [[water-binder-ratio]] · [[fly-ash]]
- High-strength ECC: chemical shrinkage is most of total; with higher S/B and W/B total shrinkage
  falls (Ye et al. 2019 [65], Fig. 12, p. 6) ⚔️ W/B direction opposite to Gao 2018 (same page).
- 20% saturated lightweight aggregate: autogenous −67%, drying −37% within 90 d, but lower ductility
  and strength (Sahmaran et al. 2009 [67], p. 6).
- Zeolite (15/20/30% sand replacement): 28-d total drying shrinkage down by > 55% (Wang et al.
  2020 [68], p. 6–7).
→ NEW [[shrinkage]]

## 4.6 Acid (p. 7)
- Nano-modified ECC, pH 1 for 28 d after 14-d curing: f'c −8 to −10% (Mansoori & Behfarnia 2019 [69]).
- Acid mine drainage: first-crack −23.7%, ultimate tensile strength −17.8%, ε −36.5% (Wu et al.
  2020 [70]).
- 3% H₂SO₄ for 6 weeks: mass loss > 8%; limestone powder reduces it (Turk & Demirhan 2017 [71]).

## 4.7 Abrasion (p. 7–8)
- Replacing fly ash with slag, silica fume, metakaolin raised abrasion resistance (metakaolin best);
  mass loss vs f'c follows a power fit y = 523.35x⁻¹·⁸⁴¹ (text says "exponential") (Mohamed et al.
  2019 [72], Fig. 13).
- Hydro-abrasion mass loss falls with PVA 0 → 2 vol.% and with curing age (Abid et al. 2018 [73],
  Fig. 14). Best at 3% PVA in Wu et al. 2018 [45] (Fig. 15).
- PE did not help; steel > PVA 8 mm > PVA 12 mm (Mohamed et al. 2018 [74]). PP-ECC better than
  PVA-ECC after 6 h (Fig. 16, ref. [73]).
- Underwater abrasion depends more on curing age than on thickness 1–3 cm (Abid et al. 2018 [75]).

## 4.8 Fatigue (p. 8–9)
- Flexural fatigue life of ECC several orders of magnitude higher than plain concrete at same stress
  (Fig. 17, ref. [80]).
- Strain hardening increased with fatigue stress level (0.23–0.65) and strain capacity reduced
  (Liu et al. 2018 [76]) ⚠️ wording ambiguous.
- Crack growth da/dN = c·ΔK_tip^m (Paris-type), fatigue life by integration (Eqs. 5–6, Qiu & Yang
  2017 [77]).
- S–N models: Sui 2018 S = 1 − 5.75×10⁻³(lgN)^2.43; Liu 2017; Suthiwarapirak 2004 PVA and PE
  bilinear models (Table 4).
- Self-healing of low pre-damage improves subsequent fatigue life (Qiu et al. 2018 [79]).
- Tensile fatigue: bridging stress drops quickly in first 600–1000 cycles (Huang et al. 2017 [80]);
  little degradation at small crack opening (XFEM, Huang & Zhang 2018 [81]).
→ NEW [[fatigue]]

## 4.9 Other (carbonation, water absorption) (p. 9)
- ECC carbonation resistance much better than plain concrete (refs. [84,85]) ⚠️ both refs are about
  ordinary concrete. Average carbonation depth 0.8 mm after 28 d in 20 ± 3% CO₂ (Zhang W. et al.
  2018 [88], PP + PVA ECC, Fig. 18; means 0.40 / 0.56 / 0.58 / 0.80 mm at about 3 / 7 / 14 / 28 d).
- MgO-based ECC with accelerated carbonation curing: longer curing (1 → 7 d) raised tensile strength
  and lowered ε (Wu et al. 2018 [86]). Printed inequality "1d > 3d > 7d" for strength contradicts
  the text "increased" ⚠️.
- Hot water raises PVA–matrix chemical bond and lowers apparent fiber strength — negative for
  long-term durability (Li et al. 2004 [14], p. 9). → [[fiber-matrix-interface]]
- 40% fly ash gave lowest water absorption; chloride permeability decreased as FA rose 0 → 100%
  (Arun Kumar & Ragul 2018 [87]). → [[fly-ash]]
- Alkaline environment named in the outlook only; ref. [17] (Sahmaran & Li 2008, alkaline) is cited
  only for the post-peak cracking statement (p. 2), not discussed.

## Mechanisms / explanations offered by authors
- ECC durability comes from micro-cracking (tight cracks) and self-healing, not density (p. 2, p. 9).
- Fiber–matrix interface changes under exposure control durability; three-phase coupling means
  component-level durability knowledge may not transfer (p. 2, p. 10).
- High temperature: fiber melting/decomposition and interface weakening remove bridging (p. 3).
- Frost: microcracks release ice-expansion stress; fiber bridging limits damage (p. 4).
- Salt: hydration products densify matrix and improve bridging in sulfate (p. 5).
- Fatigue: fiber bridging and random orientation buffer crack growth (p. 8).
- Long-term pozzolanic hydration of FA/slag/SF lowers long-term tensile ductility and self-healing
  (refs. [90,91], p. 9).

## Outlook / gaps stated by authors (§5–6, p. 9–10)
1. Most data at 28 d; lack of long-age durability data; long-term strain capacity weakens with age.
2. Durability of light-weight, high-strength, ultra-high-ductility, and high-strength high-ductility
   ECC is limited. → [[hshdc]] · [[uhdc]]
3. Durability varies with regional raw materials; multi-scale study of source effects needed.
4. Fiber–matrix interface effect on durability poorly understood at micro scale.
5. Single-exposure studies dominate; few quantitative coupled-environment studies; fiber
   durability under temperature, salt, acid and alkali less studied than composite durability.
6. Lack of field applications verifying lab results.
7. Short-term accelerated tests do not reflect long-term durability.
8. ECC used for strengthening depends on long-term interface integrity (ECC–substrate) — needs study.
   → [[rc-member-strengthening]]
9. No model describes degradation of strain hardening over service life (Conclusions, p. 10).

## Limitations (stated + observed)
- Stated: see Outlook.
- Observed: narrative review, no systematic search method; many values only in figures.
- Observed: several citation mismatches (refs. [7], [31], [41], [45], [47], [84,85]); ref. [4] year
  printed 2020 (J. Mater. Civil Eng. 12(2)) ⚠️; equation numbering jumps from (1) to (5).
- Observed: temperature (°C) symbols and µ dropped in the text layer; Fig. 4 scale bar reads
  "100 mm" (probably µm ⚠️).
- Observed: mixes, W/B, fiber dimensions and ages mostly not given; values not comparable across studies.
- Observed: "high temperature" values do not state residual vs hot testing.
