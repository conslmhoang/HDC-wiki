# HDC under combined sulfate attack and dry–wet cycles (Li, Shi & Kou, 2021)

> Re-checked against the publisher PDF (15 pp.) on 2026-10-21: page numbers added, Figs. 2 and 6
> read and cross-checked with Tables 4–5, every tabulated number confirmed.

## One-paragraph summary
Xi'an University of Technology (Shi Junping group; first author also at Shangqiu Normal University) (p. 1)
tested 100 mm cubes of a PVA-fibre, high-fly-ash **HDC** and a **28-d ordinary concrete (OC)** after
0–150 dry–wet cycles in water or in 5%, 10% or saturated (16.3% at 20 °C) Na₂SO₄ solution (§2.1, p. 3;
§2.3.2, p. 4). Each cycle = 48 h immersion + 1 h air drying + 23 h oven drying at 50 °C + cooling (§2.3.2, p. 4).
The only mechanical property measured is **uniaxial compressive strength** (Tables 4–5, pp. 6–7; Fig. 1, p. 6).
From it the authors derive a loss rate δ (Fig. 2, p. 7), a corrosion-resistance coefficient K_f = f_c,n/f_c,0
(§4.1, p. 8) and a damage variable D(n) = 1 − f_c,n/f_c,0 fitted with a quadratic in n (Eqs. 3–4, pp. 11–12;
Table 6, p. 13; Fig. 6, p. 12).
HDC f'c (initial 45.8 MPa at 90 d) generally rose up to 30 cycles and fell after 105 cycles; at 150
cycles it was 50.1 / 37.9 / 28.9 MPa in 5% / 10% / saturated solution vs 58.3 MPa in water (Table 4, p. 6).
The loss rate at 150 cycles in saturated solution was 50.3% for HDC vs 76.3% for OC (§3.2, p. 7). SEM showed
drying-shrinkage cracks, ettringite, gypsum and a loosened matrix (Fig. 4, p. 10). The authors conclude HDC
resists combined sulfate + dry–wet exposure better than OC. Tensile strain hardening is assumed from the
HDC label, not demonstrated.

## Objective / research question
- Quantify the degradation of HDC compressive strength under combined sulfate attack and dry–wet
  cycles, as a function of Na₂SO₄ concentration and number of cycles, and compare with OC (§1, p. 3, last para).
- Establish a strength-based damage variable and explain the damage mechanism using SEM (Abstract, p. 1; §4).
- Motivation: salt lakes, saline soil and collapsible loess in north-western China, with seasonal
  water-level fluctuation (§1, p. 1).

## Test methods
- Specimen geometry: 100 × 100 × 100 mm cubes; 40 groups of HDC and 40 groups of OC, 3 cubes per group (§1, p. 3; §2.2, p. 4).
- Exposure: Na₂SO₄ (anhydrous, pure) at 0% (water control), 5%, 10% and saturated (16.3% by mass at 20 °C),
  prepared at room temperature, renewed every 30 days (§2.1, p. 3; §2.3.2, p. 4).
- Dry–wet cycle: immerse 48 h → air-dry 1 h → oven-dry 50 °C for 23 h (101-type blast oven) → cool to room temperature
  (§2.3.1–2.3.2, p. 4). About 3 d per cycle (computed), so 150 cycles ≈ 450 d of exposure (computed, not stated).
- Test points: 0, 30, 60, 90, 105, 120, 135, 150 cycles (§2.3.2, p. 4).
- Loading / rate: uniaxial compression, WAW-1000 universal machine (1000 kN), 0.6 MPa/s to failure (§2.3.1, p. 4; §2.3.2, p. 5).
- Measurement: peak load only (no stress–strain curve); visual morphology (Fig. 3, p. 8); SEM (Phenom XL) (Fig. 4, p. 10).
  Mass is mentioned in §4.2 (p. 9) but **no mass data are reported**.
- Derived indices:
  - loss rate δ (§3.2, p. 5, refs [12,15]): no formula printed. δ = (f_c,water,n − f_c,sulfate,n)/f_c,water,n
    at the same n. **Confirmed**: Fig. 2a (p. 7) points (e.g. 5% ≈ −0.10 at 30 and 60 cycles; 10% ≈ 0.17 at 60,
    ≈ 0.09 at 105, ≈ 0.35 at 150; saturated ≈ 0.16 at 30, ≈ 0.50 at 150; read from Fig. 2a) match this formula
    applied to Table 4 within ≈ 0.01, and the text values 4.7%, 14.0%, 50.3%, 76.3% (p. 7).
  - corrosion-resistance coefficient K_f = f_c,n / f_c,0 (§4.1, p. 8);
  - damage variable D(n) = 1 − f_c,n / f_c,0 (Eq. 3, §4.4, p. 11), fitted as D(n) = a·n² + b·n + c (Eq. 4, p. 12; Table 6, p. 13).
- Curing & test age: exposure starts at 90 d (HDC) / 28 d (OC); 0-cycle = these ages (§3, p. 5).

Main findings (with Table/Fig. location):
- HDC f'c generally rose to 30 cycles, then fell; after 105 cycles it fell "in a straight line"
  (§3.1, p. 5; Conclusion 2, p. 13; Fig. 1a, p. 6; Table 4).
- Higher Na₂SO₄ concentration → lower maximum f'c and larger loss rate (§3.1, p. 5; §3.2, p. 7; Fig. 2a; Conclusion 3, p. 13).
- 5% solution caused no evident damage to HDC (D < 0 at all n, i.e. f'c stayed above 45.8 MPa) (§4.4, pp. 11–12; Fig. 6a; Table 4).
- 10% and saturated solutions caused serious damage; D became positive (§4.4, p. 12; Fig. 6a).
- HDC loss rate < OC loss rate at the same concentration and cycle count, e.g. 50.3% vs 76.3% at 150 cycles,
  saturated (§3.2, p. 7; Fig. 2).
- In 5% solution, HDC f'c exceeded OC f'c beyond 120 cycles (§3.1, p. 5; Fig. 1b; Tables 4–5: 56.6 vs 54.1 at 120).
- Visual (Fig. 3, p. 8; §4.1, pp. 8–9):
  - 5%, 150 cycles: HDC intact, no spalling, no crystals, no fibre pull-out, visible surface cracks;
    OC lost its cube shape with aggregate exposed.
  - 10%: HDC pitting at 60 cycles; fibres exposed at 150 cycles but shape kept. OC corner loss at 60
    cycles, surface matrix fully spalled at 150.
  - saturated: HDC fibres exposed and volume expanded at 60 cycles; corner loss and surface
    crystallisation at 150 cycles ("could not be used normally").
- Compression failure: HDC "drum shape", cracked but not scattered (ductile-like); OC two inverted cones,
  "slim waist", brittle (§4.3, p. 11; Fig. 5, p. 11).

## Mechanisms / explanations offered by authors
- **Combined physical and chemical attack** (Abstract, p. 1; §4.2, pp. 9–10):
  - Physical: drying shrinkage produces microcracks that grow with cycling (Fig. 4a, d, p. 10); Na₂SO₄·10H₂O
    crystallises from supersaturated pore solution on drying (Eq. 1, p. 10; crystallisation theory, ref. [40]).
  - Chemical: hydration products react with SO₄²⁻ to form ettringite (AFt) and gypsum (Eq. 2, p. 10; Fig. 4b, c).
    The authors state the expansion is "approximately 120%" (§4.2, p. 9, no citation) ⚠️ unverified.
- **Early strength gain**: products (gypsum, ettringite, salt crystals) fill pores, raising mass and
  f'c before about 60 cycles ("self-compacting" stage) (§4.2, p. 9; Abstract). Gypsum forms at high SO₄²⁻ and
  is said to "repair" defects (§4.2, p. 10).
- **Later loss**: more products and crystals → larger, more cracks → spalling → loss of mass and f'c;
  loose pore matrix (Fig. 4f, p. 10) (§4.2).
- **Why HDC beats OC** (§4.1, p. 9; §4.2, p. 9):
  - PVA fibres resist cracking, toughen and slow crack propagation, which delays sulfate ingress (Fig. 4e);
  - no coarse aggregate, finer particles, denser structure, smaller pores → less ettringite, lower
    expansion stress, fewer cracks.
- Sources of scatter in the damage fit: non-uniform fibre dispersion, few data points, batch variation (§4.4, p. 12).

## Limitations (stated + observed)
- Stated: fit error from uneven fibre dispersion, few data points and specimen variability (§4.4, p. 12).
- Observed:
  - **No tensile, flexural or strain-capacity test.** Strain hardening and "high ductility" are
    asserted from the HDC label, not shown. Whether ductility survives sulfate exposure is unknown.
  - **Age mismatch**: HDC starts at 90 d, OC at 28 d. Different f_c,0 (45.8 vs 51.9 MPa) and maturity
    complicate the HDC-vs-OC comparison. OC also contains coarse aggregate and has a lower W/B.
  - **Water control also gains strength** (45.8 → 63.6 MPa at 90 cycles, Table 4); with 50% fly ash,
    continued hydration plus 50 °C oven drying likely contributes to the early rise, not only pore filling
    by sulfate products (see Synthesis below). Only the 5% solution exceeds the water control (at 30–60 cycles).
  - Mass change is discussed (§4.2, p. 9) but not reported. No XRD/EDS; phase identification is by SEM
    morphology only. No expansion, porosity or sulfate-profile measurement.
  - δ formula is not printed (§3.2), though Fig. 2 confirms it; K_f for OC quoted as 0.63 (saturated, 150 cycles,
    §4.1, p. 9) does not match Table 5 (16.7/51.9 = 0.32) ⚠️.
  - The 10% series is non-monotonic (dip at 60, peak at 105), so "rise to 30, fall after 105" is a
    simplification that fits the 5% series best. The authors' own §3.1 text (p. 5) acknowledges the
    10% series "first decreased and then increased" between 30 and 90 cycles.
  - Reference [3] in the list (p. 14) is an unrelated catalysis paper (apparent citation error).
  - Fibre content given only by mass (26 g/L); vol.% not stated.

## Relation to other work
- Agrees with:
  - Liu et al. 2017 (sulfate / sulfate+chloride, ECC durable, strength rises), cited here as ref. [2] (p. 2) and
    summarised in [[ma2021-ecc-durability-review]] (p. 5): an early strength increase in sulfate.
  - The sulfate wet–dry trend reported second-hand in [[he2024-hdc-research-advances]] (p. 11–12):
    this paper is the primary source; that summary follows the authors' Conclusion 2.
- Contradicts: ⚔️ none with ingested sources. Refinement: He 2024's "rises up to 30 cycles, then decreases
  linearly after 105 cycles" holds for the 5% series and matches the authors' conclusion, but in 10%
  solution the maximum is at 105 cycles and in saturated solution the decline starts after 30 cycles
  (Table 4, p. 6).
- Extends:
  - First **primary** durability study in the wiki, and the first Chinese PVA-HDC primary data
    (normal strength, about 46 MPa at 90 d, high FA).
  - Compressive data for [[compressive-strength]]; PVA from the same product as in
    [[li2002-pva-ecc-interface-tailoring]] and [[yang2024-hshdc-uniaxial-tension]] (39 µm × 12 mm).

> Synthesis: Because the water-control HDC gains about 39% in f'c by 90 cycles (Table 4), the
> sulfate-specific effect is better judged by δ (relative to water at the same n) than by the raw
> f'c-vs-n curve. On that basis, 5% Na₂SO₄ is beneficial or neutral up to about 60 cycles, and all
> concentrations are harmful by 150 cycles.
