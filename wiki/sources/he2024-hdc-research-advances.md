# Advances in Highly Ductile Concrete Research (He et al., 2024)

## One-paragraph summary
A narrative review by authors from Power China Northwest Engineering Corp. (Xi'an) and Liaoning
University of Technology (Jinzhou). It covers 123 references (many Chinese-language) on HDC under
five headings: fiber types and basic properties (§2), static and dynamic mechanical properties
(§3), durability under sulfate attack with wet–dry cycles and under salt freeze–thaw cycles (§4),
microstructure (§5.1), and engineering applications (§5.2). The review presents no new data. Its
only quantitative tables are a generic PVA vs PE fiber property table (Table 1, after Zhang 2022)
and a steel-fiber HSFRC dataset (Tables 2–3, Song & Hwang 2004) that does **not** show strain
hardening. Headline claims: 2.0 vol.% steel fiber raises splitting tensile strength by 98.3%
(Song & Hwang 2004, as cited, Table 2); the chloride "threshold crack width" is 55–80 µm (Jang
et al. 2011, as cited, p. 10); "ordinary HDC" has a tensile strain capacity (at peak stress) of
3–5% and a tensile strength of 3–7 MPa (Conclusion 2, p. 20, **no citation given**). The review
mixes strain-hardening composites (ECC/SHCC/HDC/UHDC) with non-strain-hardening materials (SFRC,
UHPC, HSHPC, PP/glass-FRC) in the same discussion, so each value must be checked for scope.

## Objective / research question
- The authors state that HDC research is "scattered". They aim to summarise basic mechanical
  properties and the effect of wet–dry cycles, freeze–thaw cycles and salt erosion on HDC
  durability, then state the current status and future directions (Abstract, p. 1).
- Motivation: building durability problems in China (2.34 billion m² at risk, ref. [1] as cited,
  p. 1), and harsh environments in western China / Tibetan Plateau (altitude 4000 m, down to
  −45 °C, ref. [2] as cited, p. 1), plus sulfate and wet–dry environments in northwestern China (p. 10).

## Definition of HDC used by the authors (Reported)
- "High-ductility fiber concrete (HDC)" is described as a material "proposed by Professor Victor C.
  Li" of the University of Michigan "in the last century" (p. 1, citing ref. [3] = Fischer & Li
  2002 ⚠️ citation does not match the claim).
- "HDC is a new type of concrete developed based on high-performance fiber-reinforced cementitious
  composite (ECC)" (p. 2, ref. [6]); and "HDC stands for engineered cementitious composite"
  (§2.1, p. 2) ⚠️ the authors equate HDC with ECC here, but elsewhere treat it as derived from ECC
  ("developed on the basis of ECC", §4.1, p. 10).
- Characteristics stated: fine aggregate only; cement + fly ash; PVA / PE fibers; designed by
  micromechanics ("fine mechanics") and fracture mechanics with fiber bridging; tensile strain
  hardening and multiple cracking; high compressive toughness (pp. 1–2).
- Typical crack size: "the largest crack is about 100 µm" (p. 2, ref. [5] = Liu 2022 PhD thesis).
- Tensile "stress nearly 10 times higher than that of ordinary concrete" (p. 2, no number given).
- Quantitative definition: **only** in Conclusion 2 (p. 20): ordinary HDC tensile strain capacity
  (strain at peak tensile stress) **3–5%**, tensile strength **3–7 MPa**. No citation, no test
  method, no age.
- **No** standard is cited. No Chinese JGJ/T, CECS or JSCE specification is named, and no
  threshold strain or minimum Vf is given as a formal definition.
- Related sub-classes named in the review (as named by cited authors):
  - UHDC (ultra-high-ductility concrete), PE fiber, Tongji University (Yu et al.) (p. 3).
  - UHS-UHDCC: f'c > 160 MPa and PSH uniaxial tensile ductility > 6% (p. 17, refs [106,107]).
  - UHTCC (ultra-high-toughness cementitious composites, Xu S.L. group, Dalian/China): PSH with
    3% strain capacity and crack width 100 µm (p. 11, ref. [76]).
  - HSHDC (Ranade/Li, Michigan) (pp. 8–9, refs [52,66]).
  - SHCC described as "a high-ductility material" (p. 10).

## Research history (as stated)
- HDC/ECC proposed by V.C. Li, Univ. of Michigan, "in the last century"; then systematic
  research in the USA, Japan and UK, then application (p. 1).
- Recent uses: structural strengthening, vibration damping/mitigation, beam–column joints
  (p. 1, ref. [4]).
- 3D printing timeline: Pegna 1997 (cited as "Joseph"), Khoshnevis 2003/2004 contour crafting,
  then extrusion printing (pp. 4–5, refs [26–29]); 3D-printed UHDC by Ye et al. 2021 (refs [30,32]).
- First fully unreinforced UHDC building structure in China: Bamboo and Rattan Pavilion exhibition
  hall, 10th China Flora Expo, 2021 (Zhang & Huang 2023, as cited, p. 20, ref. [123]).

## Test methods
- Specimen geometry: varies by cited study. Figure 6 (p. 13, source not cited, presumably
  Yun 2013 ⚠️ unverified) shows a dumbbell tensile specimen: total length 330 mm, end width 60 mm,
  narrow width 30 mm, narrow (gauge) length 100 mm, 30 mm transitions, thickness 30 mm.
  Other tests named: four-point bending with DIC (Yu et al., Fig. 1), three-point notched bending
  (notch/depth 0.10 and 0.23), cube compression (49 sets, Deng 2020), drop-hammer impact, tensile
  impact, shake-table tests, triaxial tests, XRF chloride migration (BAW-Merkblatt MCL, Eqs. 6–9),
  ultrasonic relative dynamic modulus (Eq. 11), SEM/XRD/EDS/MIP/X-ray CT.
- Loading / rate: not reported in a unified way (quasi-static and dynamic studies summarised).
- Measurement (LVDT, DIC, ...): DIC mentioned for UHDC beams (Fig. 1); otherwise not reported.
- Curing & test age: not reported for any HDC value. Only one curing regime stated: UHPC steam
  curing at 90 °C (Gao, as cited, p. 14).

Main findings (with Table/Fig. location):
- Fibers: PE has higher strength and modulus than PVA and is hydrophobic (p. 3, Table 1). Longer
  fibers lower f'c of PVA-HDC (refs [9–13], p. 2). Tensile σ–ε of PVA-HDC has three phases:
  elastic, strain-hardening, strain-softening (Zhang J. et al., refs [14,15], p. 2).
- Single fibers (steel, PVA, basalt) raise flexural strength but lower f'c; hybrid fibers can
  offset this (p. 6; Zhang J. et al. 2021, p. 4).
- Coarse aggregate in HDC/HPC: strengths rise for 0–28% coarse aggregate, fall at 38%; flexural
  properties weaken (Xu et al. 2019, as cited, p. 6).
- 3D printing: printed UHDC slightly lower compressive/flexural/shear strength than cast, but
  higher deformability and energy dissipation in some directions; anisotropy index Eqs. (1)–(2)
  (Ye et al. 2021, refs [30,32], p. 5). ⚠️ the text says printed specimens were lower "but the
  opposite was true for the compressive strength" — internally contradictory.
- High temperature: f'c of HDC decreases with temperature; fibers melt; colour turns yellow-brown;
  cooling effect negligible at 200 °C; resting time matters at 400 °C (Deng et al. 2020, as cited,
  pp. 7–8, Figs. 3–4).
- Size effect: fiber bridging raises fracture toughness and FPZ size "by orders of magnitude" and
  the flexural size effect of UHDC becomes negligible (Ye et al. 2021, as cited, pp. 7–8).
- Impact: HSHDC slabs keep load capacity and integrity under repeated drop-hammer impacts, only
  multiple fine cracks (Ranade et al. 2017, as cited, p. 8).
- Strain rate: tensile strength of plain concrete grows 2–3× faster with rate than compressive
  (ref. [64], p. 9). HSHDC rate effects explained by a micromechanics scale-linking model
  (Eq. 5, Vf 2%); matrix toughness increase matches first-crack strength increase (Ranade et al.
  2015, as cited, pp. 9–10).
- Seismic retrofit: PVA-HDC overlay (2 vol.%) on a half-scale 2-storey URM building outperformed
  welded-wire-reinforcement overlay in shake-table tests (Deng et al. 2019, as cited, p. 9).
  HDC jacketing of cross-shaped short RC columns reduced crack width and spalling, raised
  ductility, energy dissipation and capacity (Wang et al. 2023; Deng & Zhang 2018, as cited, p. 6).
- Chloride: chloride content in cracked RC under wet–dry cycling rises markedly only for cracks
  ≥ 90 µm (worst at dry:wet 1:3) (Wu et al. 2017, as cited, p. 10). Threshold crack width for
  chloride diffusion is 55–80 µm (Jang et al. 2011, as cited, p. 10). SHCC has much lower chloride
  penetration and corrosion rate than ordinary concrete (Kobayashi et al. 2010; Paul et al. 2016,
  as cited, pp. 10–11).
- Sulfate + wet–dry: f'c of HDC rises up to 30 cycles, then decreases linearly after 105 cycles;
  higher sulfate concentration (5%, 10%, saturated) → greater loss; HDC better than ordinary
  concrete (Li L. et al. 2021, as cited, pp. 11–12, Fig. 5). Cl⁻ partly inhibits sulfate attack
  (Liu et al. 2017; Du et al. 2019, as cited, pp. 11–12). 10% Na₂SO₄ more damaging than 5% and 30%
  (Alyami et al. 2019, as cited, pp. 12–13).
- Freeze–thaw: hybrid PVA+PE SHCC (1.5 vol.%) tensile strength little changed after 300 cycles,
  but tensile strain capacity decreased, more at high W/B (Yun 2013, as cited, p. 13). Salt
  freeze–thaw degrades ECC–concrete interface shear strength; damage model Eq. (10) (Tian et al.
  2019, as cited, p. 14). Synthesis by authors: under salt freeze–thaw, tensile strain of HDC
  drops more than tensile strength (p. 15).
- Microstructure: higher silica fume and lower W/B densify the fiber–matrix ITZ and raise bond
  in UHS-UHDCC (Lei et al. 2019, p. 17).
- Applications: lightweight high-ductility ECC with AEA and lightweight filler (Zhang Z. et al.
  2019); sprayable high-ductility FRCC for RC strengthening (Huang B.T. et al. 2019); HSHD-ECC and
  high-strength steel wire + ECC for flexural strengthening of RC beams (Qin et al. 2020; Yuan F.
  et al. 2020); UHDCC steel-free frames meeting seismic code requirements (Yu K. et al. 2018);
  textile-reinforced HDC (TR-HDC) strengthening of precast hollow-core and RC slabs (Song S. et al.
  2023, 2024); unreinforced UHDC arch shell pavilion (Zhang & Huang 2023) (pp. 18–20).

## Mechanisms / explanations offered by authors
- Fiber bridging across cracks gives multiple cracking, strain hardening and fine cracks (pp. 2–3).
- Fine cracks (below the 55–80 µm threshold) slow chloride diffusion, which is the main
  durability argument for HDC (Abstract; p. 10).
- Sulfate damage = chemical (AFt/gypsum expansion, loss of alkalinity) + physical (salt
  crystallisation pressure) (pp. 11–12).
- Freeze–thaw loss of strain capacity at high W/B attributed to weak fiber–matrix bond and more
  pores (p. 13).
- High-temperature loss: water evaporation, voids and cracks, fiber melting (p. 7).
- Shorter PVA fibers (8 mm) give more bridging fibers per crack → better tensile impact
  resistance in EDGC (p. 9).

## Limitations (stated + observed)
Stated by the authors (Conclusions, pp. 20–21):
- HDC quality cannot be guaranteed on site; construction is difficult. They propose factory
  production lines.
- 3–5% strain capacity is "not sufficient" for HDC to be used as unreinforced structural material,
  especially under extreme conditions.
- 3D-printed UHDC anisotropy and interlayer bond need systematic study.

Observed (Interpreted):
- No systematic search method, inclusion criteria or comparison table of HDC tensile data.
- The key definitional numbers (3–5%, 3–7 MPa) carry no citation.
- Many cited studies are not HDC: SFRC/HSFRC (Tables 2–3), UHPC/RPC freeze–thaw, PP/glass-fiber
  concrete SEM, HSHPC biaxial strength. The abstract's 98.3% headline comes from steel-fiber
  HSFRC (Song & Hwang 2004), not from a strain-hardening HDC.
- Several citation mismatches and garbled terms (see "To verify").
- Term usage is inconsistent: HDC is called ECC in one place and "developed based on ECC" in
  another; UHDCC is expanded as "ultra-high-toughness" on p. 19 although the cited title says
  "ultra-high ductility".
- No ages, curing regimes or test methods given for the HDC values.

## Relation to other work
- Agrees with: [[ranade2015-hshdc-tensile-rate-effects]] (cited as ref. [66]: scale-linking
  model; matrix toughness rise matches first-crack strength rise; Vf 2% PE). Impact performance
  of HSHDC (Ranade et al. 2017, ref. [52]) is consistent with the motivation in
  Ranade et al. 2013 "Composite properties of HSHDC" (ACI MJ 110(4); not a wiki source).
- Contradicts: ⚔️ PE fiber properties in Table 1 (σ 2500–2800 MPa, E 70–80 GPa, fusing point
  124–138 °C, density 0.96) differ from the UHMWPE fiber used in HSHDC (σ 3000 MPa, nominal E
  100 GPa, melting 150 °C, specific gravity 0.97; Ranade et al. 2013 "Micromechanics of HSHDC" (ACI MJ 110(4); not a wiki source), Table 2).
  Likely different PE grades and generic ranges, not a true contradiction. ⚔️ "Ordinary HDC"
  ε_tu 3–5% and σ_tu 3–7 MPa vs HSHDC 3.4–4.2% at 14.5 MPa (Ranade et al. 2013 "Composite properties of HSHDC" (ACI MJ 110(4); not a wiki source),
  [[ranade2015-hshdc-tensile-rate-effects]]): HSHDC strength is about 2× the stated HDC range,
  consistent with HSHDC being a high-strength variant. ⚔️ HDC term: here "high-ductility concrete"
  is attributed to V.C. Li/ECC and used for PVA/PE composites, unlike the HSHDC-specific usage in
  the Ranade papers (see [[terminology-hdc-ecc-shcc]]).
- Extends: adds the Chinese HDC/UHDC/UHTCC literature (Xi'an Univ. of Arch. & Tech. — Deng M.K.
  group; Tongji Univ. — Yu K./Yu J. group; Dalian Univ. of Tech. — Xu S.L./Li Q.H. UHTCC group)
  and durability topics (sulfate, wet–dry, salt freeze–thaw, chloride, high temperature) not yet
  covered in the wiki.
