# Direct Tensile Properties of ECC — Review (Yu et al., 2018)

## One-paragraph summary
A Tongji University (Shanghai) review of factors that control the **direct (uniaxial) tensile**
behaviour of ECC: fiber type (PVA vs PE vs steel/PE hybrid), the fiber reinforcing index Vf·Lf/df,
specimen size and geometry (dogbone vs dumbbell vs cylinder), strain rate (10⁻⁶ to about 150 s⁻¹,
with DIF compilations), rate effects on micromechanical parameters, and the "durability" of tensile
properties (ageing to 1 year, elevated temperature to 250 °C, cyclic and fatigue loading). It gives
a summary table of ECC property ranges (Table 1) and fiber properties (Table 2). Most numbers are
second-hand. The authors conclude that fiber properties decide ECC tensile performance, that
geometry effects exist but are under-studied, that high rates change tensile properties noticeably
(ε_tu tends to fall), and that long-term and high-temperature tensile durability needs more work
(abstract, p. 346; §6, p. 359–360). The review is the same group as [[ye2021-uhdc-flexural-size-effect]]
(Tongji UHDC/UHDCC lineage, refs [62], [66]–[68], [76]).

## Objective / research question
Summarise recent progress on (i) fiber properties, (ii) specimen size/geometry, (iii) strain rate,
and (iv) long-term, high-temperature and cyclic/fatigue tensile behaviour of ECC, and give
directions for future research (p. 347).

## Test methods
- Specimen geometry (compiled, §3, p. 348–350):
  - **JSCE (2008) dogbone** [13] (Fig. 3): total length 330 mm (85 + 40 + 80 + 40 + 85), end width 60 mm,
    gauge width 30 mm, **gauge (displacement-measurement) length 80 mm**, thickness 13 mm.
  - Rokugo et al. 2007 [77]: PVA-ECC dogbones **13, 30 and 50 mm thick** (Fig. 4).
  - Kesner et al. 2003 [22]: **Ø50 mm cylinder vs 25 × 25 mm dogbone** (Fig. 5).
  - Mechtcherine et al. 2011 [43,44]: **Ø75 mm cylinder vs 24 × 40 mm dumbbell** (Fig. 6).
  - Müller & Mechtcherine 2017 [48]: dumbbell **24 × 40 × 240 mm** for fatigue (p. 357).
- Loading / rate (compiled, §4):
  - Quasi-static to 10⁻¹ s⁻¹: Yang & Li 2014 [69] (PVA-ECC, 10⁻⁵–10⁻¹ s⁻¹); Boshoff & van Zijl 2007 [3];
    Yang & Li 2012 [70] (HVFA PVA-ECC); Ranade et al. 2015 [53] (PE, 10⁻⁴–10 s⁻¹); Maalej et al. 2005 [40]
    (S/PE hybrid, 10⁻⁶–10⁻¹ s⁻¹).
  - Impact: Mechtcherine et al. 2011 [43] at 10, 25, 50 s⁻¹; split Hopkinson bar at about 150 s⁻¹
    (Mechtcherine 2011 [44]; Curosu et al. 2016 [6]).
  - Reference rate ranges (Eibl 1988 [9], as cited, p. 348): seismic 10⁻³–10⁻¹ s⁻¹; aircraft landing
    10⁻²–1 s⁻¹; hard impact 1–50 s⁻¹; hyper-velocity impact > 10² s⁻¹.
  - Fatigue: 10⁻² s⁻¹ to represent traffic loading [48] (p. 357).
- Measurement (LVDT, DIC, ...): not reported in detail (review). Single-fiber direct tension vs
  in-situ pull-out derived fiber properties are distinguished (p. 351).
- Curing & test age: mostly not reported; ageing data 10–180 d (PVA) and 28 d–1 yr (PE) (Fig. 27).

Main findings (with Table/Fig. location):
- **Fiber type** (§2, p. 347):
  - PVA is the most common fiber (dispersibility, interface) but is hydrophilic, so it needs oil coating.
  - PE is hydrophobic, needs no surface treatment, and gives higher strength. PE-ECC has **wider cracks**
    than PVA-ECC at the same Vf (no numbers given).
  - The authors' view: PVA suits moderate-strength ECC with better environmental durability; PE suits
    high-strength structural ECC (p. 347). In §6 they extend this: PE can produce both moderate- and
    high-strength ECC (p. 359).
- **Fiber reinforcing index** (Fig. 2, p. 348): σ_tu and ε_tu rise, and crack spacing and crack width fall,
  as Vf·Lf/df rises from 7.5 to 18. The authors call the result an "empirical relationship", but no
  equation is given.
- **Size / geometry** (§3, p. 348–350):
  - Rokugo 2007 [77]: thickness 13/30/50 mm had a **negligible** effect on PVA-ECC σ_tu, ε, crack width
    and crack spacing. Fig. 4 shows crack patterns at ε = 5.00% (13 mm), 2.50% (30 mm) and 3.75% (50 mm).
  - Kesner 2003 [22]: cylinders gave **lower** peak strength and strain capacity than dogbones. Reasons
    given: initial imperfections (air, shrinkage cracks), fiber orientation/alignment, and end effects.
  - Mechtcherine 2011 [43,44]: Ø75 mm cylinders had lower ductility than 24 × 40 mm dumbbells. Reasons
    given: fiber orientation and stress singularities.
  - Conclusion: "no conclusive results" on size effect; geometry "has apparent influence"; a size-independent
    model is needed (p. 359).
- **Rate, quasi-static to 10⁻¹ s⁻¹** (§4.1, p. 349):
  - PVA-ECC becomes more brittle with rate (Yang & Li 2014 [69]; Mechtcherine [43–45]; Douglas 2005 [8]).
  - ⚔️ But Boshoff & van Zijl 2007 [3] found **no significant change** in PVA-ECC ε_tu at 10⁻⁵–10⁻¹ s⁻¹ (Fig. 9).
  - High-volume FA stabilised ε_tu across 10⁻⁵–10⁻¹ s⁻¹ because it lowers the chemical bond
    (Yang & Li 2012 [70]). Long-term performance is questioned because FA pozzolanic reaction continues
    after 28 d [78].
  - PE-ECC (Ranade 2015 [53], Fig. 10): σ_fc and σ_tu rise with rate; ε_tu and crack width fall up to
    10 s⁻¹; the crack number stays about constant.
  - S/PE hybrid (Maalej 2005 [40], Fig. 11): negligible rate effect on ε_tu; σ_tu rises significantly
    from 10⁻⁶ to 10⁻¹ s⁻¹.
- **DIF compilation** (Fig. 12, p. 349, 353):
  - Cracking-strength and tensile-strength DIFs rise moderately and nearly linearly between 1 and 10 s⁻¹,
    then rise much more steeply at higher rates, for both PE- and PVA-ECC.
  - PVA-ECC is more rate-sensitive than PE-ECC in strength **and** in strain capacity.
  - **ε_tu DIF decreases** with rate for both. Fig. 12c axis tops out at a DIF of 3.
- **Impact rates** (§4.2, p. 349–350):
  - PVA-ECC at 25 and 50 s⁻¹: marked hardening after first crack but **only one crack**. The authors warn that
    ε then depends on gauge length [43].
  - PVA-ECC at about 150 s⁻¹ (Hopkinson bar): DIF of tensile strength **6.7** and of specific fracture energy **2.4** [44].
  - PE-ECC (Curosu 2016 [6]): moderate strength, DIF σ_tu **5.4** and fracture energy **3.3**; high strength,
    **3.12** and **4.2**. The lower strength DIF and higher energy DIF are attributed to more multiple cracking.
  - Fiber failure mode: quasi-static PVA pull-out lengths were 0.2–0.5 mm and most fibers ruptured; dynamic
    pull-out lengths reached 6 mm (half the fiber length) and most fibers pulled out (Figs. 14–15). This
    contradicts single-fiber pull-out tests showing more rupture at high rates [4]. No transverse plastic
    deformation was seen in PE fibers.
- **Rate effect on micromechanics** (§4.3, p. 350–351):
  - PVA (Yang & Li 2012 [70]): fiber E, fiber strength, matrix toughness and bond all rise with rate.
    From 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻¹ s⁻¹ the **PSH energy index fell 50%** and the **PSH strength index fell 10%**.
  - PE (Ranade 2015 [53]): τ0 **+14%** over 10⁻⁴–10 s⁻¹; β **doubled**; f **+27%**; µ **+43%**; fiber
    strength **+21%**; fiber modulus **+85%**. Together these lower J′b and hence ε_tu (Fig. 21).
  - Single-fiber pull-out (Curosu 2016 [6], 2 mm embedment, 0.0025 vs 25 s⁻¹): in a 24 MPa matrix most PVA
    fibers ruptured after short pull-out (Fig. 22). PE in a matrix of about **140 MPa** (100 mm cube) gave a
    plateau or slight hardening at 25 s⁻¹ vs softening at quasi-static (Fig. 23); a few fibers still
    fractured. ⚠️ Fig. 23 caption says "normal strength matrix" while the text says high-strength matrix.
  - Single-fiber tension (Figs. 24–25): PVA is linear-elastic to rupture; PE is markedly non-linear.
  - DIFs from direct single-fiber tension [6] exceed those from in-situ pull-out [53,70]; modulus is more
    rate-sensitive than strength; PVA is more rate-sensitive than PE (Fig. 26).
- **Ageing** (§5.1, p. 352): PVA-ECC ε peaks early, then falls slowly as the chemical bond grows, to about
  3% at 180 d [26]. Loss of the oil coating could raise the bond further, but this is unstudied. PE-ECC
  showed no ε loss from 28 d to 1 yr [62].
- **High temperature** (§5.2, p. 352–353, Fig. 28):
  - Mechtcherine 2012 [45], PVA-ECC **in situ**: at 60 °C, ε rose and σ fell (lower bond); at 100 and 150 °C
    both fell sharply; at 150 °C strain hardening and multiple cracking were lost. Residual properties
    also fell from 60 to 150 °C.
  - Silva 2015 [42]: residual ε about unchanged to 90 °C, almost nil at 250 °C.
  - Bhat 2014 [2]: σ and ε fell from 100 to 600 °C.
  - Yu J. 2015 [63], HVFA PVA-ECC: residual σ and ε **rose** at 50 and 100 °C and fell at 200 °C.
  - No general trend overall, but tensile strain is almost gone after 250 °C.
  - **No PE-ECC high-temperature tensile data existed** at the time.
- **Cyclic / fatigue** (§5.3, p. 355–357):
  - Kesner 2003 [22]; Jun & Mechtcherine 2010 [15,16]: tension–compression cycling does **not** reduce ε_tu
    if compression stays below f'c. Above f'c, σ_tu and ε_tu fall (orthogonal compressive cracks). Partly
    pulled-out fibers that cannot retract give a low-stiffness zone. Stiffness falls with cycle number.
  - Single PVA fiber under cycling: irreversible strain of 41% of the total [15].
  - Müller & Mechtcherine 2017 [48]: the number of cycles falls with a higher upper stress level, smaller
    strain increments, and a switch from pure tension to tension–compression.
  - The authors warn that quasi-static cyclic results may **overestimate** seismic-rate behaviour.

## Mechanisms / explanations offered by authors
- **Hydrophilic PVA:** the strong chemical bond leads to premature fiber rupture and makes PVA-ECC sensitive
  to raw materials and mix proportions. **Hydrophobic PE:** no chemical bond, and its high strength gives a
  large margin of J′b over J_tip, so tensile behaviour is stable short- and long-term (p. 359).
- **Rate:** rising fiber modulus, strength, bond and matrix toughness lower J′b and the PSH indices, which
  lowers ε_tu. PE's lack of chemical bond makes it less rate-sensitive (§4.3).
- **Geometry:** cylinders vs dogbones differ because of imperfections, fiber orientation (2-D vs 3-D),
  stress singularities and end effects (§3).
- **Ageing:** continued hydration raises PVA chemical bond and lowers ductility; PE is not affected (§5.1).
- **Temperature:** at moderate temperature, a lower bond raises ε; near and above the melting point the fibers
  are lost (§5.2).

## Limitations (stated + observed)
- Stated: the size/geometry effect is insufficiently studied; long-term data beyond 180 d (PVA) and 1 yr (PE)
  are lacking; oil-coating durability is unknown; no PE-ECC high-temperature data exist; pull-out at high rate
  needs more study (p. 351, 352, 359–360).
- Observed:
  - Narrative review with no search method and no consistent data extraction.
  - Table 1 ranges are uncited, and strain is called "ultimate tensile strain" (peak vs failure not defined).
  - The Fig. 2 and Fig. 27b data are the authors' own and are not fully referenced (Fig. 2 has no reference).
  - ECC, SHCC and HSHDC are used as near-synonyms (Michigan HSHDC is called "high strength PE-ECC").
  - Fig. 11 caption swaps panels (panel (a) axis = strain capacity DIF, caption says tensile strength).
  - The text cites "Fig. 32b" for compressive cracking, which is shown in Fig. 30b.
  - The Fig. 23 caption contradicts the text on matrix strength.
  - The Fig. 28 caption says "after exposed" but includes in-situ data [45].
  - The PVA melting point (273 °C) is uncited.

- Contradicts: ⚔️

    Boshoff & van Zijl 2007: no significant change), p. 349.
  - Within the review: the PVA fiber failure mode at high rate (composite: pull-out dominates; single-fiber
    pull-out tests: more rupture), p. 350.
  - Within the review: the size effect (Rokugo: thickness negligible; Kesner/Mechtcherine: geometry significant).
    Not a direct conflict, because thickness and shape are different variables.
  - PE melting point 150 °C here vs 124–138 °C in [[he2024-hdc-research-advances]] and 135–145 °C in
    [[su2023-rubber-modified-hshdc]] (already listed in [[gaps]]).
  - Dumbbell geometry: the JSCE dogbone here is 80 mm gauge and 13 mm thick, vs the
    [[he2024-hdc-research-advances]] Fig. 6 dumbbell with 100 mm gauge and 30 mm thickness.
- Extends: the strain rate range up to about 150 s⁻¹ (beyond the 10 s⁻¹ maximum in the wiki's primary
  sources); ageing; elevated temperature; cyclic and fatigue tension; PVA-ECC rate data.
