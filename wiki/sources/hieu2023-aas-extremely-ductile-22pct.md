# Alkali-activated slag composite with 22% tensile strain (Lương et al., 2023)

> Naming note: the first author's family name is **Lương** (Quang-Hiếu Lương); cite as "Lương et al. (2023)". The slug uses
> "hieu" (part of the given name) by the human's choice, to match the raw file name.

## One-paragraph summary
**Experimental** study from Chonnam National University (Gwangju, Korea; corresponding author B.Y. Lee) with Daegu University —
the same group as [[choi2016-pe-alkali-activated-slag-composite]]. One **cement-free** mix (GGBS activated by powdered Ca(OH)₂,
**silica sand/binder 0.8**, crumb rubber (CR, ≈ 400 µm) at 5% of binder, 1.75 vol.% PE 18 mm × 12 µm) was produced with three
**mixing procedures** (M0 = earlier procedure of Lương et al. 2021; M1 = longer, faster mixing; M2 = fibres dry-mixed with the
solids) and a new curing regime that includes 24 h at 80 °C (Tables 1, 3; Fig. 3; §2.2). At 28 d, f'c was 52.2–54.6 MPa and
nearly independent of mixing (COV 1.83%) (Fig. 7). All mixes strain-hardened with saturated multiple cracking. **R5-S8-M1 reached
a tensile strain capacity of 22.34 ± 0.48% at a tensile strength of 7.63 MPa**, and M2 20.89%, vs 9.81% for M0 (Table 4). Crack
spacing was ≈ 1 mm, but **average crack width (computed at peak) exceeded 200 µm** for M1/M2 (212 / 204 µm), about twice that of
earlier highly ductile composites (Table 6, §3.1). A flexure specimen deflected 103 mm (Fig. 12). Using material sustainability
indicators, M1 had 30–34% lower embodied energy and ≈ 38–39% lower carbon footprint than an HS-ECC and the earlier rubberised AAS
composite (§4).

> Synthesis: this is the highest tensile strain capacity in the wiki (previous highs: UHDC ≈ 9.45%, Choi 2016 7.50%). It is
> obtained with **identical mix proportions** across M0–M2, so the 2.3× jump is attributed by the authors to mixing (fibre
> dispersion/homogeneity) — an unusually clean demonstration that processing, not only micromechanical design, controls ε.
> The price is wide cracks (> 200 µm), outside the "≈ 100 µm" ECC band that underpins most durability arguments.

## Objective / research question
- Show the feasibility of an alkali-activated slag composite with **ε_ts > 20%** while using a "moderate" (practical) amount of
  silica sand (S/B 0.8, vs 0–0.4 in previous AAS composites), compensated by crumb rubber (§1, last paragraphs).
- Compare three mixing procedures and a new curing procedure; compare with earlier ECC/AAS/geopolymer composites via fcεts and
  ftsεts indexes, with steel-bar extensibility (ASTM, ISO), and evaluate sustainability (MSIs) (§1, §3, §4).

## Materials & mix design

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R5-S8-M0 | 0 | 0 | binder = GGBS 0.90 + Ca(OH)₂ 0.10; crumb rubber 0.05 | silica sand 0.8 (100% passing ≈ 0.3–0.5 mm, read from Fig. 2 ⚠️) | 0.18 (0.26 incl. water in SP) | 0.106 liquid PCE (0.031 solids); defoamer 0.001 | PE | 18 | 12 | 1.75 |
| R5-S8-M1 | same | same | same | same | same | same | PE | 18 | 12 | 1.75 |

Units of binder quantities: mass ratio to binder (GGBS + Ca(OH)₂) (Table 1, "proportion by weight except for fiber"); fibre by volume.
- The three mixes differ **only** in mixing procedure (§2.2.1, Fig. 3):
  - **M0** (procedure of Lương et al. 2021): dry solids (binder, CR, sand) 1 min at 90 rpm → water + HRWRA 1 min 90 rpm + 3 min
    180 rpm → PE fibre 15 min at 270 rpm.
  - **M1**: dry 3 min 180 rpm → water + HRWRA 3 min 270 rpm + 7 min 360 rpm → PE 3 min 270 rpm + 15 min 360 rpm.
  - **M2**: solids **and PE fibre dry-mixed** 5 min 180 rpm → water + HRWRA 3 min 270 rpm + 20 min 360 rpm.
  - 18 L planetary mixer (Fig. 4).
- GGBS: specific surface 4030 cm²/g, specific gravity 2.91; CaO 42.88%, SiO₂ 34.95%, Al₂O₃ 13.58%, MgO 3.58%, SO₃ 2.52% (XRF,
  Table 2). Activator: Ca(OH)₂ powder only (no sodium sulfate, unlike Choi 2016).
- Crumb rubber: average particle size 400 µm, density 1.13 g/cm³, from waste tyres (§2.1, Fig. 1).
- PE fibre: d 12 µm, L 18 mm, 2700 MPa, 88 GPa, 0.97 g/cm³ (Table 3).
- SP solids = 29% of SP mass; high SP dose needed for rheology and fibre dispersion; it delayed hydration (§2.1, §2.2.2).
- Fresh: homogeneity and mini-slump flow shown (Fig. 5); no slump value reported.

## Test methods
- **Specimen geometry:** dumbbell (JSCE HPFRCC recommendation), 330 mm long, 13 mm thick, 60 mm wide ends, R25 transitions,
  80 mm gauge (Fig. 6a); gauge-section width not dimensioned in Fig. 6 ⚠️ (30 mm in the JSCE specimen of Choi 2016). Five
  dumbbells per mix. Compression: three 50 mm cubes per mix (ASTM C109).
- **Loading / rate:** 20 kN UTM, displacement control, 0.1 mm/min (§2.3).
- **Measurement:** load cell; two LVDTs over the 80 mm gauge. Cracks counted with a magnifier after the test; spacing = gauge /
  crack count; **average crack width = elongation at tensile strength / crack count** (all deformation assumed in cracks) (§2.3).
  ε_ts and E_t are taken **at the highest tensile stress** (§3.1; Fig. 8 shows post-peak softening beyond ε_ts).
- **Density:** Archimedes, saturated cubes (Eq. 1).
- **Bending demonstration:** one M1 plate-type specimen in a bending rig (Fig. 12); geometry, span and load not reported.
- **Curing & test age:** 11 d in air at 23 ± 3 °C (delayed demoulding), 12 d in water at 23 ± 3 °C, **24 h oven at 80 °C**, then
  air at 23 ± 3 °C to **28 d**; all tests at 28 d (§2.2.2).
→ [[dogbone-uniaxial-tension]], [[heat-curing]]

## Key results
| Mix ID | f'c (MPa) | First-crack stress (MPa) | Tensile strength (MPa) | Tensile strain capacity (%) | Crack width (µm) | Other |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R5-S8-M0 | 54.6 (50 mm cube) | 5.08 ± 0.74 | 7.92 ± 1.09 | 9.81 ± 2.92 (at peak) | 117.2 ± 11.1 (avg, computed at peak) | 66.5 ± 17.3 cracks; spacing 1.27 mm; E_t 0.64 MPa·m/m; ρ 2.264 g/cm³; fts/fc 14.50% |
| R5-S8-M1 | 53.6 (Fig. 7; 53.4 in Table 7 ⚠️) | 4.70 ± 0.38 | 7.63 ± 0.23 | **22.34 ± 0.48** (at peak) | 212.0 ± 33.2 | 86.4 ± 13.5 cracks; spacing 0.95 mm; E_t 1.38; ρ 2.261; fts/fc 14.24% |
| R5-S8-M2 | 52.2 | 4.12 ± 0.21 | 8.19 ± 0.39 | 20.89 ± 2.13 (at peak) | 204.0 ± 11.2 | 81.8 ± 5.3 cracks; spacing 0.98 mm; E_t 1.29; ρ 2.249; fts/fc 15.68% |

All at 28 d (Fig. 7; Tables 4–6). f'c values are read from data labels in Fig. 7.

Main findings (with Table/Fig. location):
- **Mixing governs ductility at fixed proportions:** ε_ts of M1 / M2 = 2.28 / 2.18 × M0; E_t ≈ 2 × M0; COV of fts and ε_ts much
  lower for M1/M2 (Table 4, §3.1). Authors attribute this to better homogeneity and fibre distribution, not measured directly.
- **f'c insensitive to mixing:** 52.2–54.6 MPa, SD 0.98 MPa (COV 1.83%); density ≈ 2.25–2.26 g/cm³ (SD 0.006) (Fig. 7, §3.1).
- **First-crack strength falls** M0 → M1 → M2 (5.08 → 4.12 MPa), which the authors link to f'c (§3.1, Table 4).
- **fts/fc 14.2–15.7%**, ≈ 1.5× normal concrete (< 10%) (Table 5, §3.1).
- **Cracking:** more cracks ↔ higher ε (M1 most cracks, 86.4, and highest ε); spacing 0.95–1.27 mm ("saturated"); average
  computed width 117 µm (M0) vs **204–212 µm** (M1, M2), "two times higher" than previous highly ductile composites (Table 6, §3.1).
- **Bridging:** fibre pull-out with gradual exhaustion of bridging, turning to rupture when stress dominantly exceeds bridging
  capacity; good dispersion seen at the crack face (Fig. 11, §3.1).
- **Flexure:** M1 specimen reached 103 mm deflection with saturated multiple cracking (Figs. 12–13); geometry not reported.
- **Comparison (Table 7, Fig. 14):** M1 ε_ts is 2.1 / 2.5 / 5.0 × S-CR5 (Lương 2021), M26 (Choi 2016) and M1 (Lee 2012), but
  fts is lower than M26 (13.06) and S-CR5 (9.48). Best fcεts (11.9 MPa) and ftsεts (1.70 MPa) of all listed composites; ε_ts is
  64.9 / 109.4 / 63.3% higher than UB (Yu 2018, 13.55%), S-CR5 (10.67%) and UD-EGC15 (Nguyễn 2021, 13.68%) (§3.1).
- **Steel extensibility:** 22.34% exceeds minimum steel-bar elongation at fracture of 9 (ASTM A615), 14 (ASTM A706),
  14 (ISO 6935-2 Class A–C) and 17% (ISO 6935-2 Class D) (Fig. 15).
- **Sustainability (MSI per m³, Table 8, Fig. 16):** M1 vs HS-ECC (Luo 2019) / S-CR5: embodied energy −30.1 / −34.3%, carbon
  footprint −39.1 / −38.2%, material cost −13.9 / −3.7% (§4). PE fibre dominates cost (≈ 893 $/m³ of the ED-AASC total, Fig. 16c read).

## Mechanisms / explanations offered by authors
- Longer/faster mixing (M1) or dry pre-mixing fibres (M2) increases matrix homogeneity and fibre distribution → more cracks,
  higher ε_ts and lower COV (§3.1, citing Li & Li 2013 on dispersion and COV).
- Crumb rubber compensates for the higher matrix toughness/cracking strength caused by S/B 0.8; earlier work found CR at 5% of
  binder raised ε by 36% (Lương 2021, as cited, §1). → [[crumb-rubber]], [[matrix-fracture-toughness]]
- Sand raises stiffness and volume stability and lowers shrinkage and cost vs sand-free mixes (§1, §2.1) — not measured here.
- fts > fcr is cited as the minimum condition for multiple cracking (§3.1). → [[strain-hardening]]
- Delayed demoulding (11 d) caused by the high SP dose delaying hydration (§2.2.2).
- Sustainability gains come from the large sand fraction (low EE/CF) and the cement-free binder; CR has high CF but is waste (§4).

## Limitations (stated + observed)
- Stated: average crack widths > 200 µm, about twice those of earlier highly ductile composites (Conclusion 2).
- Only **one mix proportion**; no sand-free / rubber-free control under the new mixing and curing, so the separate effects of
  sand, CR, curing (80 °C step) and mixing on the jump from earlier work cannot be separated. The M0 baseline also used the new curing.
- Fibre dispersion not measured (no image analysis, rheology or slump values); the mixing mechanism is inferred.
- No micromechanical tests (τ0, Km, J′b); the PSH argument is only fts > fcr.
- Crack widths are computed loaded averages at peak; maximum and residual widths not reported. No durability, shrinkage or
  self-healing tests despite > 200 µm cracks.
- Thin (13 mm) dumbbells with 18 mm fibres favour 2-D fibre orientation; ε_ts likely an upper bound for thick members
  (cf. [[kanakubo2006-dfrcc-tensile-test-method]]). Five specimens per mix.
- Comparison with steel extensibility mixes a composite strain at peak over an 80 mm gauge with steel elongation at fracture
  (different gauge definitions); the steel figure is a minimum spec, not a like-for-like property.
- Bending test: single specimen, no geometry or load–deflection data.
- Internal inconsistencies: M1 f'c 53.6 (abstract, Fig. 7) vs 53.4 MPa (Table 7); S-CR5 labelled 2020 in Figs. 14, 16 but
  cited as 2021 [20]; the 2012 M1 comparator in Fig. 14 is cited to ref. [29] (a panel-manufacturing paper) whereas §1 cites
  Lee et al. 2012 [16] for the first cement-free composite (4.5%, 30.6 MPa) ⚠️.

## Relation to other work
- Agrees with: [[choi2016-pe-alkali-activated-slag-composite]] (same CNU group, same PE fibre 18 mm × 12 µm at 1.75 vol.%, same
  JSCE dumbbell and 0.1 mm/min): Table 7 quotes M26 exactly as in that source (54.8 MPa, 7.50%, 13.06 MPa, 23.8%). This paper is
  the group's later evolution: Ca(OH)₂-only activator (Choi: Ca(OH)₂ + Na₂SO₄), S/B 0.8 sand (Choi: none), 5% CR, lower W/B
  (0.18/0.26 vs 0.26–0.38), heat-cured — ε rises ≈ 3× but fts falls 13.06 → 7.63 MPa and crack width ≈ 2× (89 → 212 µm).
- Agrees with: [[su2023-rubber-modified-hshdc]] that rubber helps ductility (here only as cited from Lương 2021; no rubber
  variable in this study).
- Contradicts: ⚔️ (refinement) [[choi2016-pe-alkali-activated-slag-composite]] excluded aggregate to keep matrix toughness low;
  here S/B 0.8 sand (+ CR) is compatible with 22% strain. Not a direct conflict (different activator, curing, CR, mixing).
- Contradicts: ⚔️ [[crack-width]] synthesis that PE-based composites sit at ≈ 80–200 µm and AAS + PE at ≈ 100 µm: here 204–212 µm.
- Extends: [[tensile-strain-capacity]] (new maximum in wiki, 22.34% at peak); [[uhdc]] (Yu 2018 UB/UA quoted at 13.55 / 11.98%);
  [[alkali-activated-binder]]; [[fiber-dispersion]] (mixing procedure as a variable); [[heat-curing]].
