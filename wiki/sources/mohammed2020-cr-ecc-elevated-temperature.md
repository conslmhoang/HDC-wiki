# Crumb-rubber ECC after 100–1000 °C, acid and sulphate (Mohammed et al., 2020)

## One-paragraph summary
A Universiti Teknologi PETRONAS (Malaysia) group designed 13 "CR-ECC" mixes by a face-centred central
composite design (RSM) with two variables: crumb rubber (CR) replacing river sand at 0 / 2.5 / 5% and PVA
fiber at 0 / 1 / 2 vol.% (Table 3). Fixed binder: OPC 583 + Class F FA 700 kg/m³, water 187 kg/m³. Three
mixes with 2% PVA (M6 0% CR, M3 2.5% CR, M4 5% CR) were dried 24 h at 60 °C after 28 d curing, heated at
15 °C/min to 100–1000 °C (100 °C steps), held 1 h, cooled naturally and tested in compression (50 mm
cubes, ASTM C109) (Section 2.4). Residual strength generally fell with temperature and with CR content;
the 0% CR mix gained strength at 100 °C and dropped 34% between 400 and 500 °C; all CR mixes fell to about
11 MPa at 1000 °C (Section 3.2). **No explosive spalling occurred in any mix, even at 1000 °C**, and more
CR gave fewer visible cracks at 900 °C (Section 3.3, Figs. 5–6). The paper also reports acid (5% H₂SO₄)
weight change and sulphate (5% Na₂SO₄) length change, with RSM models (Eqs. 5–7, Tables 4–5).

> Synthesis: primary evidence that oiled PVA-ECC with FA (and crumb rubber) does not spall explosively up
> to 1000 °C (1 h, pre-dried). But only compression is tested, the numerical results are mostly in
> figures, and the mix-ratio statements are internally inconsistent (see "To verify"). Treat the
> numbers as indicative.

## Objective / research question
- Behaviour of CR-ECC at elevated temperature, extended to 1000 °C beyond the usual 600–800 °C limit
  (Section 1).
- Durability of CR-ECC under acid and sulphate attack, where the authors say literature is "almost
  non-existent" (Section 1).
- RSM models for compressive strength, acid weight loss and sulphate length change as functions of CR
  and PVA (Section 2.2).

## Materials & mix design
Constituents (Section 2.1, Tables 1–2):
- **OPC:** Type 1 (ASTM C150); SG 3.15; Blaine 325 m²/kg; CaO 61.4%.
- **Fly ash:** Class F (ASTM C618); SG 2.38; Blaine 290 m²/kg; CaO 9.79%, SiO₂ 57.01%.
- **Sand:** river sand passing No. 4 sieve (< 4.75 mm).
- **Crumb rubber:** shredded waste tyres, 0.6–2.36 mm (Fig. 1); replaces sand (by mass: 5% × 467 = 23.35
  kg/m³, computed here from Table 3).
- **SP:** modified polycarboxylate HRWR, pH 6.2, SG 1.08; stated dose 0.99% of cementitious mass.
- **PVA:** Kuraray "REC 5–15" (sic), **1.2% oil coating by weight**, SG 1.3, L 12 mm, d 40 µm, aspect
  ratio "462" ⚠️ (12/0.040 = 300), σ_f 1600 MPa, E 41 GPa (Table 2).

## Test methods
- **Specimen geometry:** 50 mm cubes (compression, acid weight); 285 × 25 × 25 mm bars (sulphate length
  change); 2 bricks 215 × 102.5 × 65 mm (efflorescence, qualitative) (Section 2.4).
- **Heating:** after 28 d curing, 24 h oven drying at 60 °C; furnace (1200 °C capacity) at 15 °C/min to
  100…1000 °C; 1 h hold; furnace off, natural cooling; control 24 °C. **Residual** test.
- **Compression:** ASTM C109. Number of specimens per temperature not stated.
- **Acid:** 5% H₂SO₄, weekly weighing for 1 month. **Sulphate:** 5% Na₂SO₄, weekly length.
- **Measurement:** visual cracking, colour; no microscopy, no crack-width measurement.
- **Curing & test age:** 28 d plus drying and heating.
- **No tension or flexure test** despite Section 2.2 listing flexural and splitting tensile strength as
  responses ⚠️.

Main findings (with location):
- **Temperature:** residual f'c falls with temperature for all three mixes; 0% CR gains at 100 °C (heat
  curing, further hydration/pozzolanic FA); 0% CR drops 34% between 400 and 500 °C; CR mixes lose little
  between 100 and 500 °C and drop sharply from 600 to 1000 °C (Section 3.2, Fig. 3).
- **Structural class:** all three mixes retain "structural concrete" strength up to 800 °C; at 1000 °C none
  does (Section 3.2, citing MacGregor).
- **Spalling and cracking:** no explosive spalling up to 1000 °C; visible deterioration only above 600 °C;
  at 900 °C more CR → fewer cracks (Section 3.3, Figs. 5–6).
- **Melting points as stated:** CR melts at 170 °C and PVA at 230 °C, creating vapour escape paths
  (Section 3.3; no citation or measurement for CR) ⚠️.
- **Colour:** grey at 24 °C → dark brown / buff at 1000 °C (Fig. 7).
- **Acid:** slight weight gain attributed to gypsum/ettringite; greatest loss at 2.5% CR + 1% PVA (Fig. 9).
- **Sulphate:** CR reduces length change; several specimens shrank (Fig. 10).
- **RSM:** compressive model R² 0.946, adj. 0.907, pred. 0.752; CR term insignificant (p 0.48), PVA and
  PVA² significant (Tables 4–5, Eq. 5).

## Mechanisms / explanations offered by authors
- PVA melting at about 230 °C opens channels that release vapour pressure and prevent explosive spalling;
  the same channels later become weak points, explaining the drop at 400–500 °C (Wu & Li 2017, as cited,
  Section 3.2).
- CR lowers strength through soft-particle strain incompatibility, entrapped air on the non-polar rubber
  surface and, when heated, melting that leaves voids (Sections 3.1–3.2).
- CR melting at about 170 °C adds vapour paths, reducing cracking at high temperature (Section 3.3).
- Above 500 °C cement paste forms porous, weak calcium silicates (Section 3.3).

## Limitations (stated + observed)
- **Observed:** no tension, flexure or crack-width test; "ECC" status not demonstrated.
- **Observed:** stated W/B 0.32 conflicts with Table 3 (187/1283 = 0.146); 0.32 equals w/c.
- **Observed:** stated SP 0.99% conflicts with Table 3 (1.30–1.60% of binder).
- **Observed:** Section 2.2 describes the variables as "paste aggregate ratio and curing regime" and lists
  flexural and splitting responses — apparently text from another study.
- **Observed:** Section 3.1 says strength rises with CR, then says CR decreases strength; Conclusion 2 cites
  a 100 °C gain "at 0% and 1% CR" though only 0 / 2.5 / 5% CR were heated.
- **Observed:** residual values for most temperatures are given only in Fig. 3; n per test not stated.
- **Observed:** specimens were pre-dried 24 h at 60 °C, which reduces spalling risk compared with moist specimens.
- **Observed:** 50 mm cubes are small for spalling assessment.
