# Crumb-rubber ECC after 100–1000 °C, acid and sulphate (Mohammed et al., 2020)

> Re-checked against the publisher PDF (17 pp.) on 2026-10-21; locations now give PDF page numbers, and the residual
> strengths and acid weight losses were read from Figs. 3 and 8.

## One-paragraph summary
A Universiti Teknologi PETRONAS (Malaysia) group designed 13 "CR-ECC" mixes by a face-centred central
composite design (RSM) with two variables: crumb rubber (CR) replacing river sand at 0 / 2.5 / 5% and PVA
fiber at 0 / 1 / 2 vol.% (Table 3, p. 5). Fixed binder: OPC 583 + Class F FA 700 kg/m³, water 187 kg/m³. Three
mixes with 2% PVA (M6 0% CR, M3 2.5% CR, M4 5% CR) were dried 24 h at 60 °C after 28 d curing, heated at
15 °C/min to 100–1000 °C (100 °C steps), held 1 h, cooled naturally and tested in compression (50 mm
cubes, ASTM C109) (Section 2.4, p. 5). Residual strength generally fell with temperature and with CR content
(24 °C: ≈ 75 / 58 / 46 MPa for 0 / 2.5 / 5% CR, read from Fig. 3, p. 7); the 0% CR mix gained strength at 100 °C
(≈ 79 MPa) and dropped 34% between 400 and 500 °C (≈ 69 → 45 MPa); the CR mixes fell to ≈ 11–13 MPa at 1000 °C
(Section 3.2, p. 6–7). **No explosive spalling occurred in any mix, even at 1000 °C**, and more
CR gave fewer visible cracks at 900 °C (Section 3.3, p. 8, Figs. 5–6, p. 9). The paper also reports acid (5% H₂SO₄)
weight change and sulphate (5% Na₂SO₄) length change, with RSM models (Eqs. 5–7, Tables 4–5, p. 12–13).

> Synthesis: primary evidence that oiled PVA-ECC with FA (and crumb rubber) does not spall explosively up
> to 1000 °C (1 h, pre-dried). But only compression is tested, most numbers are figure read-offs, and the
> mix-ratio statements are internally inconsistent (see "To verify"). Treat the numbers as indicative.

## Objective / research question
- Behaviour of CR-ECC at elevated temperature, extended to 1000 °C beyond the usual 600–800 °C limit
  (Section 1, p. 3).
- Durability of CR-ECC under acid and sulphate attack, where the authors say literature is "almost
  non-existent" (Section 1, p. 2).
- RSM models for compressive strength, acid weight loss and sulphate length change as functions of CR
  and PVA (Section 2.2, p. 4).

## Materials & mix design
Constituents (Section 2.1, p. 3, Tables 1–2, p. 3):
- **OPC:** Type 1 (ASTM C150); SG 3.15; Blaine 325 m²/kg; CaO 61.4%, SiO₂ 20.76%.
- **Fly ash:** Class F (ASTM C618); SG 2.38; Blaine 290 m²/kg; CaO 9.79%, SiO₂ 57.01%, Al₂O₃ 20.96%, LOI 1.25%.
- **Sand:** river sand passing No. 4 sieve (< 4.75 mm).
- **Crumb rubber:** shredded waste tyres, 0.6–2.36 mm (Fig. 1, p. 4); replaces sand (by mass: 5% × 467 = 23.35
  kg/m³, computed here from Table 3).
- **SP:** modified polycarboxylate HRWR, pH 6.2, SG 1.08, free chloride 0.1%; stated dose 0.99% of cementitious mass (p. 3).
- **PVA:** Kuraray "REC 5–15" (sic), **1.2% oil coating by weight**, SG 1.3, L 12 mm, d 40 µm, aspect
  ratio "462" ⚠️ (12/0.040 = 300; 462 would match d ≈ 26 µm), σ_f 1600 MPa, E 41 GPa (Table 2, p. 3).

Units of binder quantities: kg/m³ (Table 3, p. 5; values confirmed in the PDF). Fiber mass not listed (2 vol.% ≈ 26 kg/m³ at SG 1.3, computed here).
Design: the 13 mixes are a face-centred CCD; M5, M9, M11, M12, M13 are identical centre points (2.5% CR, 1% PVA).

Ratios computed here (by mass): FA/C 1.20; sand/binder 0.36; water/cement 187/583 = 0.32. **W/B check (PDF,
2026-10-21):** Table 3 (p. 5) prints water 187, OPC 583, FA 700 kg/m³ for all 13 mixes, giving W/B = 187/1283 =
**0.146**; Section 2.3 (p. 4–5) states "a constant water binder ratio of 0.32". The PDF confirms both values as printed.
0.32 equals w/c exactly, so the stated "W/B" is most likely w/c; the paper never resolves it ⚠️ (unresolved, keep both).
A volume check of Table 3 (SG from Tables 1–2, sand SG 2.65 assumed) sums to only ≈ 0.88 m³ per "m³" with 187 kg water,
and ≈ 1.10 m³ if water were 0.32 × 1283 = 411 kg, so Table 3 itself may be mis-scaled (computed here) ⚠️.
SP 16.68–20.53 kg/m³ = 1.30–1.60% of binder (2.9–3.5% of cement), not the 0.99% stated ⚠️.

Mixing: dry 2 min (OPC, FA, CR, sand) → water + SP, 5–8 min → PVA, 5 min (Hobart mixer, Section 2.4, p. 5).
Curing: 28 d; regime (water / moist) not stated ⚠️ (confirmed absent in the PDF).

## Test methods
- **Specimen geometry:** 50 mm cubes (compression, acid weight); 285 × 25 × 25 mm bars (sulphate length
  change); 2 bricks 215 × 102.5 × 65 mm (efflorescence, qualitative) (Section 2.4, p. 5).
- **Heating:** after 28 d curing, 24 h oven drying at 60 °C; furnace (1200 °C capacity) at 15 °C/min to
  100…1000 °C; 1 h hold; furnace off, natural cooling; control 24 °C. **Residual** test (p. 5).
- **Compression:** ASTM C109. Number of specimens per temperature not stated.
- **Acid:** 5% H₂SO₄, weekly weighing for 1 month (7–28 d). **Sulphate:** 5% Na₂SO₄, weekly length (p. 5).
- **Measurement:** visual cracking, colour; no microscopy, no crack-width measurement.
- **Curing & test age:** 28 d plus drying and heating.
- **No tension or flexure test** despite Section 2.2 listing flexural and splitting tensile strength as
  responses ⚠️ (p. 4).

Main findings (with location):
- **Temperature:** residual f'c falls with temperature for all three mixes; 0% CR gains at 100 °C (≈ 75 → 79 MPa;
  heat curing, further hydration/pozzolanic FA); 0% CR drops 34% between 400 and 500 °C; CR mixes lose little
  between 100 and 500 °C (2.5% CR: 39.81/47.66 = 83%; 5% CR: 91%) and drop sharply from 600 to 1000 °C (Section 3.2,
  p. 6–7, Fig. 3).
- **Not stated in the text but visible in Fig. 3:** both CR mixes *lose* strength between 24 and 100 °C (≈ 58 → 48
  and ≈ 46 → 42 MPa); their "83% / 91%" retention is relative to 100 °C, not to 24 °C (vs 24 °C: ≈ 69% and ≈ 83% at
  500 °C). Above ≈ 500 °C the three curves converge (≈ 21–25 MPa at 800 °C), so the CR penalty largely disappears.
- **Structural class:** all three mixes retain "structural concrete" strength up to 800 °C; at 1000 °C none
  does (Section 3.2, p. 7, citing MacGregor). At 1000 °C strength is "about 20%" of room temperature (p. 7);
  Fig. 3 gives ≈ 21–24%.
- **Spalling and cracking:** no explosive spalling up to 1000 °C; visible deterioration only above 600 °C;
  at 900 °C more CR → fewer cracks (Section 3.3, p. 8, Figs. 5–6, p. 9).
- **Melting points as stated:** CR melts at 170 °C and PVA at 230 °C, creating vapour escape paths
  (Section 3.3, p. 8; no citation or measurement for CR) ⚠️.
- **Colour:** grey at 24 °C → dark brown / buff at 1000 °C (Fig. 7, p. 9).
- **Acid:** the authors state a "slight weight gain" attributed to gypsum/ettringite (p. 9–10; Conclusion iv), but
  Fig. 8 shows a gain only for M4; all other mixes lost 0.2–7.3%. Greatest loss at 2.5% CR + 1% PVA (Fig. 9, p. 10).
  Table 5 gives the weight-loss model mean as −3.11 (sign opposite to the Fig. 8 bars) ⚠️.
- **Sulphate:** more PVA increases and CR reduces length change; several specimens shrank (Fig. 10, p. 11; Conclusion v).
- **RSM** (28 d, Tables 4–5, p. 12–13; Eqs. 5–7, p. 12): compressive model R² 0.9458, adj. 0.907, pred. 0.7519;
  CR term insignificant (p 0.4792), PVA (p 0.0013), CR×PVA (p 0.0356) and PVA² (p 0.0001) significant.
  Eq. 5: f'c = 57.444 + 2.78016 CR − 8.54238 PVA − 1.23 CR·PVA − 0.25438 CR² + 8.2057 PVA² (CR, PVA in %).
  Weight-loss model R² 0.9528 / adj. 0.9191 / pred. 0.7399 (CR² only significant term; B² p 0.2337 labelled
  "significant" in Table 4); length-change model R² 0.9699 / 0.9483 / 0.8324 (all terms significant).
  The text (p. 11) says "crumb rubber and temperature are significant", which contradicts Table 4 and the fact that
  temperature is not a model variable.

## Mechanisms / explanations offered by authors
- PVA melting at about 230 °C opens channels that release vapour pressure and prevent explosive spalling;
  the same channels later become weak points, explaining the drop at 400–500 °C (Wu & Li 2017, as cited,
  Section 3.2, p. 7; ref. 41 = Wu & Li 2017, CFRP-ECC hybrid).
- CR lowers strength through soft-particle strain incompatibility, entrapped air on the non-polar rubber
  surface and, when heated, melting that leaves voids (Sections 3.1–3.2, p. 6–7).
- CR melting at about 170 °C adds vapour paths, reducing cracking at high temperature (Section 3.3, p. 8).
- Above 500 °C cement paste forms porous, weak calcium silicates (Section 3.3.1, p. 9).

## Limitations (stated + observed)
- **Observed:** no tension, flexure or crack-width test; "ECC" status not demonstrated.
- **Observed:** stated W/B 0.32 conflicts with Table 3 (187/1283 = 0.146); 0.32 equals w/c. Confirmed in the PDF.
- **Observed:** stated SP 0.99% conflicts with Table 3 (1.30–1.60% of binder).
- **Observed:** Section 2.2 describes the variables as "paste aggregate ratio and curing regime" and lists
  flexural and splitting responses — apparently text from another study.
- **Observed:** Section 3.1 says strength rises with CR, then says CR decreases strength; Conclusion 2 cites
  a 100 °C gain "at 0% and 1% CR" though only 0 / 2.5 / 5% CR were heated.
- **Observed:** residual values for most temperatures are given only in Fig. 3 (read off here); n per test not stated.
- **Observed:** acid conclusion ("slight weight gain") does not match Fig. 8 (losses up to ≈ 7%); centre-point
  replicates scatter 4.8–7.3%.
- **Observed:** specimens were pre-dried 24 h at 60 °C, which reduces spalling risk compared with moist specimens.
- **Observed:** 50 mm cubes are small for spalling assessment.

- **Contradicts:** ⚔️ none directly in the wiki.

  ECC loses tensile SH beyond 200 °C with about 40% lower tensile strength (ref. 19, which is Phan 2008 on
