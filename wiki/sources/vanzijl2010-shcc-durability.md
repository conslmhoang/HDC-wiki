# On durability of SHCC (van Zijl & Wittmann, 2010)

## One-paragraph summary
Invited JACT paper summarising the RILEM TC 208-HFC Subcommittee 2 State-of-the-Art Report on the
durability of **SHCC** (2005–2009). It asks whether the micro-scale crack widths of SHCC translate
into durability (slower ingress of moisture, gas, chloride), and whether the small crack widths
survive sustained, cyclic and rate-varying loads. Key messages: average crack width stays about
constant (about 40–60 µm) over a wide strain range and is insensitive to strain rate (Fig. 2), but
the **maximum** crack width grows with strain (Fig. 1b, Fig. 4b) and under sustained load at 80% of
σ_tu cracks become fewer and wider (p. 263). A crack width of **about 100 µm** appears to be the
threshold beyond which water permeability rises by orders of magnitude, and the authors state the
same threshold was found for chloride diffusion (Sahmaran et al. 2007, as cited; Fig. 5, p. 264).
Yet the **tolerable crack width for a given aggressive environment "is yet to be determined"** (p. 264).
Chloride exposure lowers PVA chemical bond and MOR but not matrix toughness; leaching halves matrix
toughness and MOR; PVA fibres resist alkali and acid; SHCC passes ASTM C666 freeze–thaw and ASTM C672
salt scaling but fails the RILEM CDF limit (2.2 vs 1.5 kg/m²); 26-week hot-water ageing lowers ε_tu
from about 4.5% to 2.75%. The authors conclude that crack-width **distribution** (probability of wide
cracks), not the average, must govern service-life design, and that only part of the strain capacity
should be used in aggressive environments.

## Objective / research question
- Scrutinise whether crack-width limitation in SHCC "translates into increased durability through
  retardation of ingress of moisture, gas and other deleterious matter" (Abstract, p. 261).
- Check whether small crack widths are kept under sustained, cyclic or other load paths (Abstract).
- Summarise the RILEM TC 208-HFC SC2 report (van Zijl & Wittmann 2010, Springer) (Abstract, p. 262).

## Definitions used by the authors (Reported)
- SHCC: resists increased tensile stress after first crack over a significant strain range; tensile
  strain "up to and beyond 3%" vs about 0.015% for conventional cement-based materials (p. 261).
- HPFRCC is classed by tensile strength and ductility into UHPFRC and SHCC (Fig. 1a, p. 261).
- f'c: SHCC **25–60 MPa**; UHPFRC **180–240 MPa** (p. 261). Both with 1% ≤ Vf ≤ 3% (p. 261).
- Durability mechanism differs: UHPFRC relies on a dense matrix (low capillary suction); SHCC on
  crack-width control (p. 261). → [[terminology-hdc-ecc-shcc]] · [[uhpc-vhsc]]

## Test methods
- Specimen geometry: not reported (review).
- Loading / rate: cited tests include monotonic uniaxial tension at strain rates 10⁻⁵–10⁻¹ s⁻¹
  (Adendorff 2010, Fig. 2); force- and deformation-controlled cyclic tension (Jun & Mechtcherine 2007;
  Adendorff 2010); tensile creep of specimens pre-cracked to 1% strain at 30/50/70/80% of σ_tu
  (Boshoff 2007) (Fig. 3, p. 263).
- Measurement (LVDT, DIC, ...): crack-width distributions (Fig. 1b); neutron radiography of water
  uptake (Zhang 2010a); single-fibre pull-out; nano-indentation; small notched beams.
- Durability methods cited: water permeability (Lepech & Li 2005); chloride diffusion under bending
  (Sahmaran 2007); wet–dry chloride exposure of loaded beams (Miyazato & Hiraishi 2005); accelerated
  electrochemical corrosion (Sahmaran 2008); ASTM C1260; ASTM C666A; ASTM C672; RILEM CDF (TC 117-FDC);
  NH₄NO₃ / KNO₃ leaching.
- Curing & test age: mostly not reported; hot-water ageing after 28 d at 60 °C curing (Li 2004a).

Main findings (with Table/Fig. location):
- **Crack width vs strain rate**: average crack width about 36–59 µm at 1/2/3% strain for rates
  10⁻⁵–10⁻¹ s⁻¹, "negligible sensitivity" (Fig. 2, p. 263; values read from figure ⚠️).
- **Cyclic load**: average crack width stays about 50–65 µm up to 3% strain under monotonic and
  cyclic tension, but the **maximum** crack width rises from about 140 µm to about 290–420 µm
  (Fig. 4b, Adendorff 2010; read from figure ⚠️). → [[crack-width]]
- **Sustained load** (Boshoff 2007): new cracks form during creep, but fewer than under monotonic
  loading to the same strain; at 80% of σ_tu, larger spacing and **wider** cracks (p. 263).
  Fibre pull-out creep and rate effects are possible sources of time-dependent crack width (Boshoff 2009a,b).
- **Water permeability**: about 100 µm crack width "appears to be a threshold" beyond which
  permeability rises by several orders of magnitude (Fig. 5a, Lepech & Li 2005, p. 264). → [[permeability]]
- **Chloride diffusion**: "This threshold [about 100 µm] was also found for chloride diffusion by
  Sahmaran et al. (2007)" (p. 264, Fig. 5b). Fig. 5b actually plots D_eff vs **beam deformation**
  (mm), not crack width: ECC (M45) linear y = 24.87x + 2.04 (R² 0.95), mortar ln y = 2.31 + 5.19x³
  (R² 0.99), D in 10⁻¹² m²/s (Fig. 5b).
- **Neutron radiography**: cracks well below 100 µm are water-filled within a few minutes of contact
  (Zhang 2010a, p. 264). → [[permeability]]
- **Tolerable crack width** of SHCC for a given aggressive environment "is yet to be determined" (p. 264).
- **R/SHCC vs R/mortar** (Miyazato & Hiraishi 2005): under load + wet–dry chloride, one 0.3 mm crack
  in R/mortar vs several 0.1 mm cracks in R/SHCC; chloride penetration depth lower and corrosion
  rate slower in R/SHCC (p. 265).
- **Accelerated electrochemical corrosion** (Sahmaran et al. 2008): SHCC extended the corrosion
  propagation period and kept beam load capacity better (p. 265).
- **Chloride on interface** (Kabele 2006, 2007): 10 cycles of 5 d saturated NaCl at 20 °C + 2 d oven
  at 50 °C → chemical bond "significantly reduced", frictional bond slightly increased; matrix
  nano-properties and matrix fracture toughness practically unaffected; MOR reduced (p. 265).
  → [[fiber-matrix-interface]]
- **Leaching** (Němeček 2006, 2007; Kabele 2006, 2007): 1.4 mol/L KNO₃ for 16 d, or 3–6 mol/L NH₄NO₃
  for 70 d → lower elastic modulus, much lower pull-out force, matrix fracture toughness and MOR
  both down by almost 50% (p. 265–266).
- **Fibre durability**: PVA kept nearly initial tensile strength after 14 d in alkaline water at
  80 °C; dilute H₂SO₄/HCl/HNO₃ for > 1 year → ≤ 5% strength loss (p. 266). → [[pva-fiber]]
- **ASR**: SHCC like Ref. 6 showed no expansion after 30 d in ASTM C1260 (Sahmaran & Li 2008),
  probably non-reactive silica sand; high FA volume makes ASR unlikely (p. 266). → [[fly-ash]]
- **Sulfate**: not studied experimentally for SHCC; FA "may be useful" (postulated, p. 266).
- **Freeze–thaw ASTM C666A** (Li et al. 2003, non-air-entrained): concrete removed after 110 cycles
  (5 weeks); SHCC survived 300 cycles with no measurable loss of dynamic modulus; durability factor
  10 vs 100; ε_tu about 3% after 300 cycles, same as wet-cured control (p. 267).
- **Salt scaling ASTM C672** (Sahmaran & Li 2007): after 50 cycles, SHCC (FA/C up to 2.2), even
  pre-cracked, within acceptable limits; mortar severely deteriorated (p. 267).
- **RILEM CDF test**: SHCC mass loss **2.2 kg/m² after 28 cycles** > accepted limit **1.5 kg/m²**
  (Setzer & Auberg 1995) → insufficient by this method, though better than mortar (p. 267).
- **High temperature**: thermal decomposition PP 271 °C, PVA 263 °C; PP melts > 170 °C; spalling
  typically 190–250 °C; polymeric FRCC (Vf 0.5–0.75%, not SHCC) delayed internal heating vs plain
  concrete and SFRC (Yoshitake 2006, p. 266). → [[high-temperature-behaviour]]
- **Shrinkage**: conventional concrete εs ≈ 0.05–0.08% > εu ≈ 0.015%; SHCC shrinkage at
  40% < RH < 60% strains it into the multi-cracking region (Fig. 6, Wang & Li 2006); autogenous
  shrinkage of Ref. 6-type SHCC 0.033% in the first 2 d, 0.072% at 47 d; setting about 11 h (p. 267).
  → [[shrinkage]]
- **Hot-water ageing** (Li et al. 2004a): 26 weeks, 60 °C-cured composites; fibre properties little
  changed; frictional bond and apparent fibre strength unchanged; chemical bond about +50%; σ_tu up,
  ε_tu about 4.5 → 2.75%; treatment estimated to correspond to > 70 years of service (p. 268).
- **MOR of PVA-SHCC** after 4 weeks in 60 °C water: insignificant loss vs about 30% loss for GRC
  (Horikoshi 2006, p. 268).

## Mechanisms / explanations offered by authors
- Ingress mechanisms: capillary suction of matrix, transport by fine cracks, migration along
  fibre–matrix interfaces (p. 261).
- Crack-width distribution, not a single number, arises from non-uniform fibre dispersion (p. 262).
  → [[fiber-dispersion]]
- Wider cracks are more efficient transport paths; probability of a critical maximum crack width
  should be treated with extreme-value statistics (p. 263).
- Chloride: SHCC delays depassivation and slows each corrosion stage; cover spalling "highly
  unlikely" because of SHCC ductility, but the deterioration mechanisms "cannot be avoided" (p. 265).
- Reduced MOR after chloride exposure most likely due to lower bridging effectiveness (p. 265).
- Freeze–thaw resistance possibly due to air introduced with the fibres, to be confirmed (p. 266–267).
- Combined loads (mechanical + frost + chemical) accelerate diffusion-driven processes; frost
  coarsens pore structure (Table 2, p. 268).

## Limitations (stated + observed)
- Stated: tolerable crack width per environment unknown; time-dependent crack width under sustained
  load not yet measured accurately; combined loads not studied; early-age cracking of large SHCC
  elements not measured; sulfate and AAR with reactive aggregate not tested; retardation of heating
  by polymeric fibres needs testing and simulation (p. 263–268).
- Observed: short review; few numbers beyond Table 1; most quantitative values only in figures.
- Observed: the "100 µm chloride threshold" attributed to Sahmaran 2007 is not visible in Fig. 5b,
  which uses deformation (mm) not crack width ⚠️.
- Observed: Table 1 PE fibre diameter "12 µm" is unusually small for PE ⚠️; "Water" row is
  water/cement, not W/B.
- Observed: hot-water ageing "> 70 years" equivalence is an estimate, with no basis given (p. 268).
