# Recent Progress on HPFRCC in Japan (Kunieda & Rokugo, 2006)

## One-paragraph summary
An invited review (Nagoya Univ. / Gifu Univ.) that links the **required performance** of HPFRCC
to its **field applications in Japan** around 2003–2005. It places HPFRCC in the JCI ductility
classification (cement composites ⊃ FRCC ⊃ DFRCC ⊃ HPFRCC, Fig. 3), lists the tentative contents
of the forthcoming JSCE recommendation "Design and Construction of HPFRCC Structures (Draft)"
(Table 1), proposes a **bilinear design tension model** with the initial cracking strength divided
by a material factor of 1.3 (Fig. 4, p. 21), and discusses fatigue, bond in HPFRCC/RC and
HPFRCC/steel composites, crack elongation over existing cracks, penetration resistance, aesthetics,
shrinkage and erosion (§2). Section 3 describes six application types: the Mihara Bridge steel-deck
overlay (40 mm, about 800 m³), ECC coupling-beam dampers in high-rise buildings, sprayed dam
facing (Mitaka Dam), irrigation channels, an ASR-damaged retaining wall with 24-month crack
monitoring, and a railway viaduct. The main message is that Japanese uses rely mostly on the
**fine multiple cracking** (durability, shielding, aesthetics), not on tensile load capacity,
because design concepts that use deformation capacity were still missing, and that quantitative
performance criteria are not yet defined (p. 20, §4).

## Objective / research question
Introduce recent HPFRCC applications in Japan, organised by the required performance that each
application relies on, to give a basis for evaluating design concepts (abstract; p. 20).

Main findings (with Table/Fig. location):
- **Classification** (Fig. 3, p. 20, from JCI 2003b): FRCC (e.g. FRC) ⊃ DFRCC (ductile in compression,
  tension and bending; includes bending-only multiple cracking) ⊃ HPFRCC (strain hardening in tension, e.g. ECC).
- **Required performance vs application** (Table 2, p. 20): bridge decks → tensile capacity, fatigue, bond,
  repair minimisation; building dampers → tensile capacity (energy absorption), repair minimisation; dams,
  irrigation channels, retaining walls, viaducts → crack-based properties (crack elongation, penetration
  protection, aesthetics, durability). Fine cracks dominate the Japanese demand (p. 20).
- **Design tension model** (Fig. 4, p. 21; JSCE 2005a): bilinear curve defined by initial cracking strength
  and strain at tensile strength; initial cracking strength ÷ **material factor 1.3** (JSCE draft).
- **Fiber orientation** depends on specimen geometry and process (thin sections, spraying, extrusion → 2-D
  orientation), so design tensile properties should reflect actual conditions (p. 21).
- **Fatigue** (Matsumoto 2003, as cited, p. 21): HPFRCC S–N is bilinear on a semi-log plot vs linear for FRC;
  longer lives at high stress levels but **shorter lives than ordinary FRC at low stress levels**, because only
  a few cracks propagate.
- **Bond** (p. 22–23): good bond to a substrate constrains HPFRCC and may reduce crack distribution
  (restricted ductility); an unbonded zone over an existing crack spreads cracking (Fig. 9). Bonded
  HPFRCC/steel pipe with ribs gives narrower crack spacing and higher capacity (Fig. 8).
- **Corrosion** (Hiraishi 2005, as cited, p. 24): in fine-cracked HPFRCC, rebars corrode by slow
  micro-cell rather than fast macro-cell corrosion.
- **Mihara Bridge** (Hokkaido, 972 m, central span 340 m; p. 26): in 2004, half the asphalt depth on the
  steel deck was replaced by **40 mm HPFRCC** with plate-type dowels; ready-mixed plant, agitator trucks,
  secondary site mixing; about **30 m³/day, about 800 m³ total**.
- **Mitaka Dam** (Hiroshima; p. 27): 2003, **30 m³ sprayed, 30 mm thick, 500 m²** on the upstream face;
  anchors at 1.5 m² intervals.
- **Irrigation channels** (Shiga, Toyama; p. 27): 2005, troweled / sprayed, 6 mm (walls) and 10 mm (slabs);
  spraying 2–3× faster than troweling; conventional and polymer mortars cracked within one month, HPFRCC did not.
- **Retaining wall** (Gifu; 18 × 5 m, ASR; 2003; Table 3, Figs. 15–16, p. 29): see table above; residual
  core expansion 0.005–0.011 (unit as printed ⚠️).
- **Railway viaduct** (2005; p. 30): sprayed 10 mm HPFRCC with anchors; crack width halved under cyclic
  loading; accelerated carbonation confirmed a carbonation-suppressing effect (no numbers).

## Mechanisms / explanations offered by authors
- Uniform macroscopic deformation arises from densely distributed cracks; softening localises in a few
  cracks (p. 19, Figs. 1–2).
- Low-stress fatigue weakness: only a few cracks propagate, so cracking state controls fatigue (p. 21).
- Compatible deformation with rebar lowers interface shear and splitting/spalling of the cover; a critical
  strain equal to the rebar yield strain may give uniform multiple cracking (Fischer 2002, as cited, p. 23).
- Fine cracks lower water/air permeation and hence shield, protect against carbonation and change the
  corrosion mode (p. 24, 27, 29).
- Shrinkage stress under restraint may reduce tensile load capacity even though shrinkage cracks are fine (p. 24).

## Limitations (stated + observed)
- Stated: required performance criteria and test methods for strain and other parameters are not defined
  (p. 20); impact and compressive ductility are omitted (p. 21); fatigue design needs more data (p. 21);
  no erosion test methods exist (p. 25); visual inspection of fine cracks is problematic (p. 24).
- Observed:
  - A narrative review; nearly all evidence is second-hand from Japanese-language reports (JSCE 2005a etc.).
  - No mix proportions, fiber geometry, f'c or tensile values for any field material.
  - The field HPFRCC is not identified by product or fiber, except in Table 3.
  - Field crack data have no measurement method or number of readings.
  - "Residual expansion 0.005 to 0.011" has no unit (probably %) ⚠️.
